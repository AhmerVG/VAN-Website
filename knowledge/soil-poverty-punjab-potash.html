Soil Poverty Series · No. 1

Soil Poverty: Punjab’s Potash

As potash runs short, urea and DAP buy less yield.

If potash fertiliser use in Punjab does not rise, this report’s model projects that Punjab’s area-weighted average available soil potassium will fall below 100 ppm between the crop years 2026-27 and 2043-44. In the average case, it falls below 100 ppm in 2035-36. In this report’s projections, Punjab’s average means the average available soil potassium of the soils tested in about 2016 to 2018, weighted by each district’s cultivated area (129.8 ppm at the start). The 3 fixed cases in Section 4 start instead from the unweighted average of 132.3 ppm. The average case is the median of the model runs. The worst case (2026-27) and the best case (2043-44) are the 10th and 90th percentiles of the runs: in any year, 1 in 10 runs projects a lower level than the worst case, and 1 in 10 projects a higher level than the best case. Available soil potassium is the potassium (K) extracted from soil with ammonium acetate, in milligrams per kilogram of soil (ppm), as Punjab soil laboratories report it. 100 ppm is a warning line chosen for this report. It is not an official grade (Section 1). The years above are model projections, not measurements. The most recent province-wide soil testing records used in this report are from about 2016 to 2018, when the tested samples averaged 132 ppm. Falling available soil potassium matters because, where it is low, a crop can take up less of the nitrogen and phosphorus in urea and diammonium phosphate (DAP), as Section 6 shows. Holding SFRI’s measured yearly fall constant (case A) puts the year at 2036-37, and a published US method, which is set to reproduce the same measurement, puts it at about 2021 to 2024 (Section 4). Years written as 2023-24 are crop years. The model works by crop year, so a model result given as a single year, such as 2031, is the crop year that ends in that year (2030-31). Spans of several years are written in full, as in 2003 to 2009. 2003 to 2009 means the SFRI readings of 2003 and 2009 and, for the potassium balance, the average of the crop years 2003-04 to 2008-09.

Key findings
  • In 2023-24, 5 crops in Punjab (wheat, rice, sugarcane, maize and cotton) took an estimated 940,000 tonnes of potassium, expressed as potassium oxide (K₂O), out of the soil. About 36,000 tonnes of K₂O were put back as potash fertiliser, as reported by the National Fertilizer Development Centre (NFDC). The 36,000 tonnes are less than 4% of the estimated amount taken out. With manure and irrigation water also counted, an estimated 324,000 tonnes of K₂O were put back in all.
  • In 2023-24, the estimated net potassium removal from Punjab’s soils was about 616,000 tonnes of K₂O. Net potassium removal is the potassium taken out by the 5 crops minus all the potassium put back in potash fertiliser, manure and irrigation water. The 2023-24 net removal was 1.88 times (nearly 2 times) the estimated average yearly net removal of about 328,000 tonnes of K₂O in 2003 to 2009. That period is used for comparison because it is the most recent period, among those this report found, in which the fall in Punjab’s available soil potassium was measured province-wide.
  • In about 2016 to 2018, 18% of the soil samples tested in Punjab were below 100 ppm. For 2025-26, the model estimates that about 34% of the soils represented by those samples were below 100 ppm in the average case. The estimated share is 51% in the worst case and 28% in the best case.
  • The model tested an assumed rise in Punjab’s potash fertiliser use to 416,000 tonnes of K₂O a year by 2034-35, 12 times its 2024-25 level. Across the model runs, this rise delays the year in which average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm by only about 2 years, compared with the projection in which potash fertiliser use does not rise. Potash fertiliser use of 416,000 tonnes a year would cover half of the net potassium removal projected for 2034-35; the other half would still be drawn from potassium in the soil.

The projections come from a potassium balance model built on Punjab and federal statistics. In this report, the yearly fall is the drop in available soil potassium in 1 year. It is given either in ppm a year or as a percentage a year of that year’s available soil potassium. The model takes its yearly fall from a measurement made by the Soil Fertility Research Institute (SFRI), Punjab, on its monitoring sites from 2003 to 2009. This is the only province-wide measurement of the yearly fall in Punjab that this report found. Section 4 compares the model’s results with other methods, and Section 9 sets out the model’s limitations.

1Measured soil potassium in Punjab, about 2016 to 2018

Soil samples tested in Punjab in about 2016 to 2018 had an average available soil potassium of 132 ppm, and 18% were below 100 ppm

Available soil potassium is given in milligrams of potassium (K) per kilogram of soil (ppm), extracted with ammonium acetate, as Punjab soil laboratories report it. The tested samples averaged 132.3 ppm, given as 132 ppm where rounded.

The figures come from 770,160 soil samples tested in all 36 districts of Punjab in about 2016 to 2018. These records were validated, normalised and mapped by Vital Agri Nutrients (VAN) for the Soil Atlas. They are soil testing records, not a random survey designed to represent all of Punjab’s farmland, and a field may have more than 1 sample. The averages in this report therefore describe the tested soils.

This report grades available soil potassium on VAN’s 5-band soil-test scale, the scale used in the Soil Atlas: Critical below 50 ppm, Weak 50 to 100 ppm, Average 100 to 150 ppm, Moderate 150 to 200 ppm and Healthy above 200 ppm. This report uses 100 ppm, the boundary between the Weak and Average bands, as a warning line. It is not an official grade. Of the 2016 to 2018 samples, 18% were below 100 ppm, in the Critical or Weak band. The Soil Atlas map divides Punjab into 4,911 squares of about 5 km by 5 km. In 656 of these squares, about 13%, the average available soil potassium of the 2016 to 2018 samples was below 100 ppm.

Why this report uses 100 ppm. In a study of 8 alluvial soil series in Pakistan, wheat responded to added potash wherever clay was up to 20% and available soil potassium, measured by the same ammonium acetate test, was up to 120 ppm (Ranjha, Mehdi and Qureshi 1992). A warning line at 100 ppm is therefore more likely to miss some soils on which wheat would respond to potash than to include soils on which it would not. On VAN’s 5-band scale, 100 ppm is also the boundary between the Average and Weak bands.

Grades differ between countries. There is no single world standard: each country sets its own grades for the same soil test. The US Tri-State system sets the critical level at 100 to 120 ppm, depending on soil type (Ohio State University). This report’s warning line of 100 ppm is at the lower end of that range.

2Potassium taken out by crops and put back

Punjab’s estimated net potassium removal nearly doubled, from an average of about 328,000 tonnes of K₂O a year in 2003 to 2009 to about 616,000 tonnes in 2023-24

Net potassium removal counts potash fertiliser, manure and irrigation water as potassium put back. The period 2003 to 2009 is used for comparison because SFRI measured the yearly fall in available soil potassium on its monitoring sites in those years.

Between 1971-72 and 2023-24, the potassium taken out of Punjab’s fields by the 5 crops rose from about 203,000 to about 940,000 tonnes of K₂O a year, as estimated by this report’s balance model. Wheat grain and straw (bhoosa) together account for half of this removal. Of the 5 crops, removal by maize has grown fastest over this period. On the figures this report uses (NFDC from 2016-17 onwards; see the data notes in Section 9), potash fertiliser use in Punjab did not exceed 53,000 tonnes of K₂O in any year from 1971-72 to 2023-24.

3Comparison with international estimates

International estimates are consistent with this report’s balance: Pakistan’s potassium deficit grew in every decade from the 1970s to the 2010s, and Pakistan used little potash fertiliser in 2023

The potassium balance in Section 2 is built from Pakistani government statistics. Its results are consistent with 3 international sources, each built independently of this report: a peer-reviewed potassium budget for Pakistan, data from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) on potash fertiliser use, and World Bank data on total fertiliser use.

1. A peer-reviewed potassium budget for Pakistan, 1970s to 2010s. Pathak, Fagodiya and Singh (2024, Scientific Reports 14:29136, Table 5) built this budget from FAOSTAT and national data. The table gives the paper’s estimates as decade averages, in million tonnes of potassium (as K, not K₂O) a year. The paper’s Balance column also counts potassium put back from sources other than fertiliser, so the Balance is not the amount taken out by crops minus the fertiliser figure:

DecadeTaken out by cropsFertiliserBalance

2010s1.420.03-1.09

In this budget, Pakistan’s potassium deficit grew larger in every decade. The paper puts Pakistan’s share of South Asia’s potassium deficit at between 10% and 19%, the second largest share in South Asia.

2. FAO fertiliser data. In 2023, Pakistan used 1.48 kg of potash fertiliser (as K₂O) per hectare of cropland. The world figure was 23.1 kg; Bangladesh used 63.0 kg and China 69.1 kg. In the same year Pakistan used 122.9 kg of nitrogen per hectare (FAOSTAT via Our World in Data). Calculated from these figures, Pakistan used about 83 kg of nitrogen for every 1 kg of potash fertiliser (as K₂O).

3. World Bank data. Pakistan’s total fertiliser use was 160 kg per hectare of arable land in 2023, above the world figure of 138 kg (World Development Indicators, AG.CON.FERT.ZS, via Our World in Data). In 2023, then, Pakistan used more fertiliser per hectare than the world as a whole (World Bank) but much less potash fertiliser (FAO). The 2 sources use different areas, arable land for the World Bank and cropland for FAO, so the 2 figures are not on exactly the same basis.

Comparison with this report’s Punjab model. For the 2010s, this report’s model estimates that the 5 crops in Punjab took out about 724,000 tonnes of K₂O a year and that net potassium removal was about 412,000 tonnes a year. For all of Pakistan in the 2010s, the peer-reviewed budget gives an average of 1.71 million tonnes of K₂O a year taken out and a deficit, the paper’s counterpart of net potassium removal, of 1.31 million tonnes a year. This report converted these figures from the paper’s figures in K. Most of the difference between the 2 estimates comes from their scope: the peer-reviewed budget covers every crop in all of Pakistan. It also counts about 23% of the potassium taken out by crops as put back, against about 43% in this report’s model. Because Punjab grows most of Pakistan’s crops, a Punjab estimate that covered every crop would be expected to account for most of the national deficit of 1.31 million tonnes a year. This report’s Punjab figure of about 412,000 tonnes is much smaller. It is therefore more likely to understate net potassium removal than to overstate it, for 2 reasons: it covers only 5 crops, and it counts a larger share of potassium as put back in irrigation water and manure.

In the average case, the model estimates Punjab’s average available soil potassium at about 116 ppm in 2025-26 and projects about 81 ppm in 2049-50.

The share of soil samples below 100 ppm was 18% when they were measured in about 2016 to 2018. In the average case, the model estimates that about 34% of the soils represented by those samples were below 100 ppm in 2025-26, and projects 52% in 2034-35.

What decides the year in which average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm. The most important input is how strongly the release of potassium from mica, a soil mineral, slows the yearly fall as available soil potassium drops. This input changes the projected year more than all the other inputs together. No measurement in Punjab sets the value of this input, so the model uses 2 alternative assumptions about it, each in half the runs. Under the first, which is the rule of case B below, the yearly fall is a percentage of the soil level, so it becomes smaller in step with the level of available soil potassium; in the average case of these runs, average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm in 2031. Under the second, the yearly fall becomes smaller as available soil potassium approaches a floor of 60 to 80 ppm, below which it does not fall; in the average case of these runs, it falls below 100 ppm in 2038. The year 2035-36 in the average case for all the runs rests on giving the 2 assumptions equal weight. The second most important input is the value used for the yearly fall measured by SFRI, which the runs vary by plus or minus 50%. Section 9 gives the projected year for a wider set of assumed strengths of this slowing.

Under both assumptions used in the runs, Punjab’s average available soil potassium eventually falls below 100 ppm. The model did not test any assumption under which it levels off above 100 ppm. In only about 2 of every 100 runs does it stay above 100 ppm until 2049-50.

In case B, the yearly fall in available soil potassium is a percentage of the soil level. The percentage starts at the yearly fall SFRI measured in 2003 to 2009, about 1.5% a year, and is raised or lowered each year in proportion to that year’s net potassium removal compared with the 2003 to 2009 net removal. 5Effect of more potash fertiliser

In the model, raising Punjab’s potash fertiliser use to 12 times its 2024-25 level delays the fall below 100 ppm by only about 2 years

In case C, the model assumes that Punjab’s potash fertiliser use rises steadily from the 34,000 tonnes of K₂O used in 2024-25 to 416,000 tonnes a year by 2034-35, which is 12 times the 2024-25 amount. That amount, 416,000 tonnes, would cover half of the net potassium removal projected for 2034-35, about 830,000 tonnes of K₂O. Case C is built on case B, the fixed case that gives the earliest year, so its year is compared with case B (2028-29), not with the average case of the runs (2035-36).

  • Across the model runs, the assumed increase delays the year in which Punjab’s average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm by about 2 years; in 8 of every 10 runs the delay is between 0 and 5 years. Between the fixed cases B and C, the delay is 1 year: in case C, with the increase, average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm in 2029-30; in case B, in which potash fertiliser use does not rise, it falls below 100 ppm in 2028-29.
  • In case C, Punjab’s average available soil potassium keeps falling after 2029-30 and reaches about 63 ppm by 2049-50. In case B it reaches 41 ppm, so the extra potash leaves the average 22 ppm higher. Available soil potassium keeps falling in case C because the other half of the net potassium removal is still drawn from potassium in the soil.
  • Covering the whole of the estimated 2023-24 net potassium removal with potash fertiliser would need about 616,000 tonnes of K₂O a year more than in 2023-24. Supplying 616,000 tonnes of K₂O would take about 20.5 million 50 kg bags of muriate of potash (MOP, 60% K₂O), or about 24.6 million 50 kg bags of sulfate of potash (SOP, 50% K₂O).

Punjab’s alluvial soils are rich in the minerals mica and illite, which hold potassium between their layers. As crops take out potassium that is not replaced, these minerals release some of the potassium they hold. Because of this release, available soil potassium falls more slowly than the potassium balance alone would suggest, but the release uses up the potassium held in the minerals. Weathered illite and vermiculite clays can also lock up (fix) part of any potash fertiliser applied later, so that crops cannot use it for a time (Wakeel and Ishfaq 2022).

6Effect on yield and fertiliser use

In a Mianwali wheat trial, potash fertiliser raised grain yield by 25%, and low available soil potassium can reduce how much nitrogen and phosphorus a crop takes up

In a 2-year wheat trial at the Adaptive Research Station, Mianwali, on sandy, low-potassium soil in the Thal, adding 93 kg of K₂O per hectare as potash fertiliser raised grain yield by 25%, from 3,625 to 4,533 kg per hectare (Abbas et al. 2013, Pakistan Journal of Botany).

Potassium also affects how much of the nitrogen and phosphorus in urea and DAP the crop takes up. A study on soil severely deficient in potassium, quoted by the International Food Policy Research Institute (IFPRI), found that applying nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium together raised the share of each nutrient that the crop took up, compared with applying only 2 of them:

NutrientShare taken up by the crop with 2 nutrients appliedShare taken up with N, P and K together
Nitrogen16%76%
Phosphorus1%13%
Potassium22%61%

With 2 nutrients applied means nitrogen and phosphorus only for the nitrogen and phosphorus rows, and nitrogen and potassium only for the potassium row. Source: Haerdter and Fairhurst (2003), as quoted in Ali et al. 2016, IFPRI Discussion Paper 01516. This is a single study, most likely on rice outside Pakistan, and the original has not been found. Its figures show what happens on soil severely deficient in potassium; they are an illustration, not a Punjab average.

National figures for Pakistan show more fertiliser used for each 100 kg of wheat produced, and less potash applied for each kg of nitrogen. These trends are consistent with the study above, although low soil potassium is probably only part of their cause:

  • Producing 100 kg of wheat in Pakistan took 4 kg of fertiliser nutrients in 1980-81 and 7.9 kg in 2014 (IFPRI 2016).
  • For every kg of nitrogen applied as fertiliser, Pakistan applied 0.036 kg of potash in 1985-86 and 0.007 kg in 2013-14 (IFPRI 2016).

Where a farmer’s soil is low in available potassium, the crop cannot fully use the nitrogen and phosphorus in the urea and DAP that the farmer has paid for.

7Recommendations for farmers, government and industry

Potassium should be given the same priority as nitrogen and phosphorus in soil testing, fertiliser policy and fertiliser supply

For farmers. Farmers should have their soil tested before the next sowing. Where the test shows available soil potassium below 100 ppm, especially on sandy land, part of the money spent on urea and DAP may be wasted until potash is added. On VAN’s 5-band scale, a result below 100 ppm is in the Weak or Critical band. A farmer can apply potash to a strip of the field and compare the crop on that strip with the rest of the field.

For the government. The most recent province-wide measurement of the yearly fall in Punjab’s available soil potassium that this report found was made on SFRI’s monitoring sites in 2003 to 2009. The government should restore fixed monitoring sites, re-test them every 2 to 3 years and publish the results. Fertiliser policy should give potassium the same priority as nitrogen and phosphorus.

For the fertiliser industry. Covering Punjab’s estimated 2023-24 net potassium removal would take about 616,000 tonnes of K₂O a year more than the potash fertiliser used in Punjab that year. Pakistan has no commercial potash mine, so supplying potassium on that scale would need imported potash, potash recovered within Pakistan and products that farmers can afford.

8Conclusion

Potassium is being taken out of Punjab’s soils faster than it is put back, and more potash fertiliser alone only slows the fall

Punjab’s soils still hold potassium, but the available part that crops can use is being taken out faster than it is replaced. The estimated net potassium removal nearly doubled, from an average of about 328,000 tonnes of K₂O a year in 2003 to 2009 to about 616,000 tonnes in 2023-24. The sandy Thal districts of Mianwali, Layyah and Bhakkar already had median levels below 100 ppm in about 2016 to 2018. If potash fertiliser use does not rise, the model projects that Punjab’s average available soil potassium will fall below the 100 ppm warning line in 2035-36 in the average case.

In the model, raising potash fertiliser use to 12 times its 2024-25 level delays the year in which Punjab’s average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm by only about 2 years, because half of the projected net removal would still be drawn from potassium in the soil. Falling available soil potassium matters for farmers because, where it is low, a crop can take up less of the nitrogen and phosphorus in urea and DAP. The main uncertainty is how strongly the release of potassium from soil minerals slows the yearly fall. No measurement in Punjab has settled this.

9Method, checks and limitations

How the model works, how it was checked, and its limitations

The model in 4 steps.

  1. Crop output: Punjab production of wheat, rice, sugarcane, maize and cotton, every year from 1971-72 to 2023-24.
  2. Potassium taken out: published studies give a range for the potassium in each tonne harvested. The fixed cases use figures at the low end of these ranges, and the model runs draw from wider ranges (wheat straw, or bhoosa, 10 to 25 kg of K₂O per tonne of grain; rice straw 15 to 25 kg per tonne of paddy, with 10% to 50% of the straw removed). Bhoosa and maize stover are counted as removed from the field, because they are fed to animals. Rice straw and sugarcane trash are mostly burnt in the field, so their potassium stays there. Half of the cotton sticks are counted as removed for firewood.
  3. Potassium put back: potash fertiliser (for each year), farmyard manure (of which 1/4 reaches fields, according to FAO) and canal and tube-well water.
  4. Yearly fall: 3.52 ppm a year, measured on SFRI’s monitoring sites in 2003 to 2009 (about 1.5% a year of the monitoring sites’ average available soil potassium). Except in case A, the model raises or lowers this yearly fall each year in step with that year’s net potassium removal. Half of the model runs use case B’s rule, in which the yearly fall is a percentage of the soil level, and the other half use a floor of 60 to 80 ppm (Section 4).

Starting values. Weighted by each district’s cultivated area, the 2016 to 2018 average is 129.8 ppm rather than 132.3 ppm, because some districts with low available soil potassium have large cultivated areas. Cases A to C start from 132.3 ppm, and the model runs start from the area-weighted average of 129.8 ppm.

It cannot detect a fall over time, so it cannot test the model, but it shows why districts differ.

At the 2003 to 2009 net potassium removal, the balance model gives a yearly fall in available soil potassium of about 1.5% a year; at the larger 2023-24 net removal, about 2.8% a year. The fall measured in Gujar Khan, about 2.3% a year, lies between these 2 figures. The Gujar Khan result is a weak test for 2 reasons: Gujar Khan is rainfed, and its first value is not a direct measurement but this report’s estimate from the share of samples in each grade reported by Qureshi et al. (2001).

Comparison 2: differences between districts. The 769,578 cleaned samples (of the 770,160) were compared district by district, after allowing for soil texture, organic matter and pH. If cropping alone were reducing available soil potassium, the districts where crops take out the most potassium per hectare would have the lowest levels, but they do not. Districts where crops take out more potassium per hectare have slightly higher available soil potassium: about 0.18 ppm more for each additional kg per hectare taken out. This relationship is weak and not statistically reliable. In the tests of about 2016 to 2018, Sahiwal, Multan and Rahim Yar Khan had high levels of available soil potassium; Mianwali, Bhakkar and Mandi Bahauddin had low levels.

Much of the difference between districts comes from how each soil was formed. Parent material, river silt and groundwater set each district’s starting level of available soil potassium, and soil tests from a single period cannot separate that starting level from the effect of 50 years of cropping. For this reason, the yearly fall used in this report comes only from places measured more than once, not from differences between districts. The districts whose median was below 100 ppm in about 2016 to 2018 are therefore low partly because of how their soils were formed.

Limitations of the model.

  • Only 1 province-wide measurement of the yearly fall in Punjab was found, from 2003 to 2009. The US drawdown method gives the same yearly fall only because it is set to reproduce it (Section 4). Scaling this yearly fall to the larger net potassium removal of recent years assumes that the yearly fall grows in proportion to the net removal; the model runs test this assumption. Because mica releases potassium as available soil potassium falls, the projections that allow the least slowing by soil minerals may overstate the fall.
  • The SFRI monitoring sites averaged about 240 ppm, nearly 2 times the 2016 to 2018 Punjab average of 132 ppm. How far their yearly fall applies to soils with lower available soil potassium is assumed, not measured. The SFRI figures are taken from IFPRI 2016.
  • How strongly soil minerals slow the yearly fall is an assumed range, not a measured one. Half the runs use case B’s rule, in which the yearly fall is a percentage of the soil level and so becomes smaller in step with the level of available soil potassium, and half use the assumption with a floor of 60 to 80 ppm. Weighting the 2 assumptions differently moves the average-case year anywhere between 2031 and 2038.
  • Irrigation water is the largest and least certain source of potassium put back. No measured figure for potassium in Punjab’s canal water exists. The fixed cases use the world river median (1.3 mg per litre) and count half the potassium in tube-well water; the model runs vary these amounts (see the table above). Much of the potassium in tube-well water is the fields’ own potassium, leached and pumped back up, so only part of it is a real addition of potassium to the field. In case B, a potassium content in irrigation water above the world river median used in the fixed cases moves the year in which average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm earlier rather than later, to between 2025 and 2028. This happens because the amount put back in irrigation water is held constant in every year. More potassium in water therefore lowers the 2003 to 2009 net removal and the recent net removal by the same amount, so the recent net removal becomes larger relative to the 2003 to 2009 net removal, and the yearly fall, which is scaled by that comparison, becomes faster.
  • The model does not count potassium washed out (leached) of sandy Thal soils or lost by erosion on rainfed land. The fixed cases also do not count fodder crops (berseem, sorghum, maize fodder), potato, pulses, oilseeds or vegetables; the model runs add an uncertain allowance of 0 to 600,000 tonnes of K₂O a year for fodder and other removals. The real net potassium removal is therefore larger than in the fixed cases. Potato and vegetables receive much of the potash fertiliser applied: this potash is included in the fertiliser figure, but the potassium these crops take out is not.
  • Potassium put back from manure and irrigation water is held constant at recent levels for every year back to 1971-72, so net potassium removal in early years is understated.
  • The projected share of samples below 100 ppm is estimated by lowering every 2016 to 2018 sample in step with the projected average available soil potassium for Punjab. It is not a new measurement.
  • Soil potassium figures after 2016 to 2018 are modelled. Crop production and fertiliser figures up to 2023-24 are recorded statistics, and the potassium taken out and put back is estimated from them. Except in case C, which assumes a rise in potash fertiliser use, every figure after 2023-24 shows what would happen if potash fertiliser use did not rise, and should not be read as a prediction.

Data notes. Punjab Development Statistics for 50 Years gives wheat production in 2017-18 as 16,210 thousand tonnes; Punjab Agriculture Statistics 2024 gives 19,179 thousand tonnes, which is the figure used. For potash fertiliser use in 2017-18, Punjab Development Statistics for 50 Years gives 64,800 tonnes and NFDC gives 37,000 tonnes; NFDC figures are used from 2016-17 onwards. In the Sheikhupura soil workbook, the values were shifted 1 column against the headings and were corrected.
