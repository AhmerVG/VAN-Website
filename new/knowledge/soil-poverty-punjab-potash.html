Soil Poverty: Punjab’s Potash

Where available soil potassium is low, crops can take up less of the nitrogen and phosphorus in urea and diammonium phosphate (DAP).

If potash fertiliser use in Punjab does not rise, this report’s model projects that Punjab’s average available soil potassium will fall below 100 ppm between the crop years 2026-27 and 2043-44. Available soil potassium is the potassium (K) extracted from soil with ammonium acetate, as Punjab soil laboratories report it, in milligrams per kilogram of soil (ppm). The 100 ppm level is a warning line chosen for this report. It is not an official grade (Section 1). In the model’s average case, Punjab’s average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm in 2035-36. The average case is the median of the model runs. In any year, half of the model runs project a lower level of available soil potassium than the average case. In the projections, Punjab’s average is the average available soil potassium of the soils tested in about 2016 to 2018, weighted by each district’s cultivated area. The years 2026-27, 2035-36 and 2043-44 are model projections. Falling available soil potassium matters because a crop can take up less of the nitrogen and phosphorus in urea and DAP where available soil potassium is low (Section 6). Besides this report’s model, 2 other methods give different years for the fall of Punjab’s average available soil potassium below 100 ppm (Section 4). The Soil Fertility Research Institute, Punjab (SFRI) measured a yearly fall in available soil potassium on its monitoring sites in 2003 to 2009. Case A holds that measured yearly fall constant. Case A gives the year 2036-37. A published US method gives a crop year of about 2020-21 to 2023-24. That method is set to reproduce SFRI’s measured yearly fall.

Key findings
  • In 2023-24, wheat, rice, sugarcane, maize and cotton took an estimated 940,000 tonnes of potassium, expressed as potassium oxide (K₂O), out of Punjab’s soils. About 36,000 tonnes of K₂O were put back as potash fertiliser, as reported by the National Fertilizer Development Centre (NFDC). The 36,000 tonnes are less than 4% of the estimated amount taken out. With manure and irrigation water also counted, an estimated 324,000 tonnes of K₂O were put back in all.
  • Net potassium removal is the potassium taken out by the 5 crops minus all the potassium put back in potash fertiliser, manure and irrigation water. In 2023-24, Punjab’s estimated net potassium removal was about 616,000 tonnes of K₂O. The 2023-24 net potassium removal was 1.88 times the estimated average yearly net potassium removal of about 328,000 tonnes of K₂O in 2003 to 2009. Net potassium removal therefore nearly doubled between these 2 periods. The period 2003 to 2009 is used for comparison because it is the most recent period, among those this report found, in which the fall in Punjab’s available soil potassium was measured province-wide.
  • In about 2016 to 2018, 18% of the soil samples tested in Punjab had available soil potassium below 100 ppm. For 2025-26, the model estimates that about 34% of the soils represented by those samples were below 100 ppm in the average case. The estimated share is 51% in the worst case and 28% in the best case. The worst case is the 10th percentile of the model runs. The best case is the 90th percentile of the model runs.
  • The model tested an assumed rise in Punjab’s potash fertiliser use to 416,000 tonnes of K₂O a year by 2034-35. That amount is 12 times Punjab’s potash fertiliser use in 2024-25. Across the model runs, this rise delays the year in which Punjab’s average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm by only about 2 years, compared with the projection in which potash fertiliser use does not rise. The delay is short because 416,000 tonnes of K₂O a year would cover only half of the net potassium removal projected for 2034-35. The other half would still be drawn from potassium in the soil.

The projections come from a potassium balance model built on Punjab and federal statistics. In this report, the yearly fall is the drop in available soil potassium in 1 year. It is given either in ppm a year or as a percentage a year of that year’s available soil potassium. The model takes its yearly fall from the measurement that SFRI made on its monitoring sites in 2003 to 2009. That measurement is the only province-wide measurement of the yearly fall in Punjab that this report found. Section 4 compares the model’s results with other methods. Section 9 sets out the model’s limitations. The rest of this paragraph explains how this report writes years. Years written in the form 2023-24 are crop years. The model works by crop year. A model result given as a single year is the crop year that ends in that year. For example, 2031 means the crop year 2030-31. Spans of several years are written in full, as in 2003 to 2009. For the SFRI readings, 2003 to 2009 means the readings of 2003 and 2009. For the potassium balance, 2003 to 2009 means the average of the crop years 2003-04 to 2008-09.

1Measured available soil potassium in Punjab, about 2016 to 2018

Available soil potassium in the soil samples tested in Punjab

Soil samples tested in Punjab in about 2016 to 2018 averaged 132 ppm of available soil potassium. Unrounded, the average is 132.3 ppm. Of these samples, 18% had available soil potassium below 100 ppm. Available soil potassium is given in milligrams of potassium per kilogram of soil (ppm), after extraction with ammonium acetate, as Punjab soil laboratories report it.

The figures come from 770,160 soil samples tested in all 36 districts of Punjab in about 2016 to 2018. These records were validated, normalised and mapped by Vital Agri Nutrients (VAN) for the Soil Atlas. The records come from soil testing and were not designed as a random survey of all of Punjab’s farmland. A field may have more than 1 sample in the records. The averages in this report therefore describe the tested soils.

This report grades available soil potassium on the 5-band soil-test scale that VAN uses in the Soil Atlas. The 5 bands are Critical below 50 ppm, Weak from 50 to 100 ppm, Average from 100 to 150 ppm, Moderate from 150 to 200 ppm and Healthy above 200 ppm. This report uses 100 ppm as a warning line. The warning line is not an official grade. The 18% of the 2016 to 2018 samples that were below 100 ppm fell in the Critical or Weak band. The Soil Atlas map divides Punjab into 4,911 squares of about 5 km by 5 km. In 656 of these squares, or about 13% of them, the average available soil potassium of the 2016 to 2018 samples was below 100 ppm.

Why this report uses 100 ppm. In a study of 8 alluvial soil series in Pakistan, wheat responded to added potash wherever clay was up to 20% and available soil potassium, measured by the same ammonium acetate test, was up to 120 ppm (Ranjha, Mehdi and Qureshi 1992). A warning line at 100 ppm is therefore more likely to miss some soils on which wheat would respond to potash than to include soils on which it would not. On VAN’s 5-band scale, 100 ppm is also the boundary between the Weak and Average bands.

Grades differ between countries. Each country sets its own grades for the same soil test. The US Tri-State system sets the critical level at 100 to 120 ppm, depending on soil type (Ohio State University). This report’s warning line of 100 ppm is at the lower end of that range.

In about 2016 to 2018, the measured median available soil potassium ranged from 81 ppm in Mianwali, the lowest, to 184 ppm in Sahiwal, the highest. The table gives the median for the 5 districts with the lowest median and for the district with the highest:

Apart from these repeat surveys, the evidence for the years before the soil testing records of 2016 to 2018 consists of published statements by experts. 2Potassium taken out by crops and put back

Punjab’s potassium balance, 1971-72 to 2023-24

Punjab’s estimated net potassium removal nearly doubled, from an average of about 328,000 tonnes of K₂O a year in 2003 to 2009 to about 616,000 tonnes in 2023-24. Net potassium removal counts potash fertiliser, manure and irrigation water as potassium put back. The period 2003 to 2009 is used for comparison because SFRI measured the yearly fall in available soil potassium on its monitoring sites in those years.

Between 1971-72 and 2023-24, the potassium taken out of Punjab’s fields by the 5 crops rose from about 203,000 to about 940,000 tonnes of K₂O a year, as estimated by this report’s balance model. Wheat grain and straw (bhoosa) together account for half of the potassium taken out by the 5 crops. The potassium taken out by maize has grown faster over this period than the potassium taken out by any of the other 4 crops. Against the potassium taken out by the 5 crops, potash fertiliser use in Punjab did not exceed 53,000 tonnes of K₂O in any year from 1971-72 to 2023-24, on the figures this report uses. From 2016-17 onwards, those figures are NFDC’s (Section 9, data notes).

By this report’s estimate, manure puts back about 85,000 tonnes of K₂O a year. Irrigation water puts back about 203,000 tonnes of K₂O a year. Each of these amounts is more than potash fertiliser put back in any year. The balance holds these 2 amounts constant for every year from 1971-72 (Section 9).

3Comparison with international estimates

International estimates of Pakistan’s potassium balance and fertiliser use

3 international sources are consistent with the potassium balance in Section 2. The balance in Section 2 is built from Pakistani government statistics. Each international source was built independently of this report. The 3 sources are a peer-reviewed potassium budget for Pakistan, data from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) on potash fertiliser use, and World Bank data on total fertiliser use.

1. A peer-reviewed potassium budget for Pakistan, 1970s to 2010s. Pathak, Fagodiya and Singh (2024, Scientific Reports 14:29136, Table 5) built this budget from FAOSTAT and national data. The table gives the paper’s estimates as decade averages, in million tonnes of potassium a year. The table’s figures express potassium as K. The potassium balance in Section 2 uses K₂O. 1 tonne of potassium as K is about 1.2 tonnes when expressed as K₂O. The paper’s Balance column also counts potassium put back from sources other than fertiliser. For that reason, the Balance does not equal the amount taken out by crops minus the fertiliser figure. In the paper’s estimates, Pakistan’s potassium balance fell from -0.37 million tonnes of K a year in the 1970s to -1.09 million tonnes of K a year in the 2010s:

In this budget, Pakistan’s potassium deficit grew larger in every decade. The paper puts Pakistan’s share of South Asia’s potassium deficit at between 10% and 19%. Pakistan’s share is the second largest in South Asia.

2. FAO fertiliser data. In 2023, Pakistan used 0.60 kg of potash fertiliser (as K₂O) per acre of cropland (1.48 kg per hectare). The figure was 9.3 kg per acre (23.1 kg per hectare) for the world, 25.5 kg per acre (63.0 kg per hectare) for Bangladesh and 28.0 kg per acre (69.1 kg per hectare) for China. In the same year, Pakistan used 49.7 kg of nitrogen per acre of cropland (122.9 kg per hectare; FAOSTAT via Our World in Data). Calculated from these figures, Pakistan used about 83 kg of nitrogen for every 1 kg of potash fertiliser (as K₂O).

3. World Bank data. Pakistan’s total fertiliser use was 65 kg per acre of arable land (160 kg per hectare) in 2023, above the world figure of 56 kg per acre (138 kg per hectare; World Development Indicators, AG.CON.FERT.ZS, via Our World in Data). In 2023, Pakistan therefore used more fertiliser per acre than the world as a whole (World Bank). Pakistan’s potash fertiliser use per acre was much lower than the world’s (FAO). The 2 sources divide fertiliser use by different areas, arable land for the World Bank and cropland for FAO. The 2 figures are therefore not on exactly the same basis.

Comparison with this report’s Punjab model. For the 2010s, this report’s model estimates that the 5 crops in Punjab took out about 724,000 tonnes of K₂O a year. It estimates Punjab’s net potassium removal in the 2010s at about 412,000 tonnes of K₂O a year. For all of Pakistan in the 2010s, the peer-reviewed budget gives an average of 1.71 million tonnes of K₂O a year taken out by crops. It gives a deficit of 1.31 million tonnes of K₂O a year. The paper’s deficit corresponds to this report’s net potassium removal. This report converted the 1.71 and 1.31 million tonnes from the paper’s figures in K. Most of the difference between the 2 estimates comes from their scope. The peer-reviewed budget covers every crop in all of Pakistan. This report’s model covers 5 crops in Punjab. The 2 estimates also differ in the share of the potassium taken out that they count as put back, about 23% in the peer-reviewed budget against about 43% in this report’s model. Because Punjab grows most of Pakistan’s crops, a Punjab estimate that covered every crop would be expected to account for most of the national deficit of 1.31 million tonnes a year. This report’s Punjab figure of about 412,000 tonnes is much smaller. The Punjab figure is smaller for 2 reasons. First, it covers only 5 crops. Second, it counts a larger share of potassium as put back in irrigation water and manure. This report’s figure is therefore more likely to understate Punjab’s net potassium removal than to overstate it.

4Projected available soil potassium to 2050

Projections of Punjab’s average available soil potassium to 2049-50

If potash fertiliser use does not rise, the model projects that Punjab’s average available soil potassium will fall below 100 ppm between the crop years 2026-27 and 2043-44. This range runs from the worst case to the best case of the model runs. The worst case is the 10th percentile of the runs. It gives the earliest year in the range, 2026-27. The best case is the 90th percentile of the runs. It gives the latest year, 2043-44. In any year, 1 in 10 runs projects a lower level of available soil potassium than the worst case. Another 1 in 10 runs projects a higher level than the best case. The average case is the median of the runs. In the average case, the model estimates Punjab’s average available soil potassium at about 116 ppm in 2025-26. It projects about 81 ppm in 2049-50.

The share of soil samples below 100 ppm was 18% when they were measured in about 2016 to 2018. In the average case, the model estimates that about 34% of the soils represented by those samples were below 100 ppm in 2025-26. It projects that the share will reach 52% in 2034-35.

What decides the year in which Punjab’s average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm. The most important input is how strongly the release of potassium from mica, a soil mineral, slows the yearly fall as available soil potassium drops. This input changes the projected year more than all the other inputs together. No measurement in Punjab sets the value of this input. The model therefore uses 2 alternative assumptions about it. Each assumption is used in half of the runs. The first assumption is the rule of case B, described below. Under this rule, the yearly fall is a percentage of that year’s available soil potassium. The yearly fall therefore becomes smaller in step with the level of available soil potassium. In the average case of the runs that use this assumption, Punjab’s average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm in 2030-31. Under the second assumption, the yearly fall becomes smaller as available soil potassium approaches a floor of 60 to 80 ppm. Available soil potassium does not fall below that floor. In the average case of the runs that use this assumption, Punjab’s average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm in 2037-38. The year 2035-36 in the average case for all the runs rests on giving the 2 assumptions equal weight. Section 9 gives the projected year for a wider set of assumed strengths of the slowing by soil minerals. The second most important input is the value used for the yearly fall measured by SFRI. The runs vary that value by plus or minus 50%.

Under both assumptions used in the runs, Punjab’s average available soil potassium eventually falls below 100 ppm. The model tested only assumptions under which Punjab’s average available soil potassium can fall below 100 ppm. Punjab’s average available soil potassium stays above 100 ppm until 2049-50 in only about 2 of every 100 runs.

5Effect of more potash fertiliser

A rise in potash fertiliser use in the model

In the model runs, raising Punjab’s potash fertiliser use to 12 times its 2024-25 level delays the year in which Punjab’s average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm by only about 2 years. In 8 of every 10 runs, the delay is between 0 and 5 years. The assumed rise takes Punjab’s potash fertiliser use from the 34,000 tonnes of K₂O used in 2024-25 to 416,000 tonnes a year by 2034-35. The 416,000 tonnes would cover half of the projected 2034-35 net potassium removal of about 830,000 tonnes of K₂O.

  • In fixed case C, the model assumes that Punjab’s potash fertiliser use rises steadily from 34,000 tonnes of K₂O in 2024-25 to 416,000 tonnes a year by 2034-35. Case C is built on case B, the fixed case that gives the earliest year. The year in case C is therefore compared with the year in case B, 2028-29. It is not compared with the average case of the model runs, 2035-36. In case C, Punjab’s average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm in 2029-30, against 2028-29 in case B. Between the fixed cases B and C, the delay is therefore 1 year.
  • In case C, Punjab’s average available soil potassium keeps falling after 2029-30. It reaches about 63 ppm by 2049-50, against 41 ppm in case B. The extra potash fertiliser therefore leaves Punjab’s average available soil potassium 22 ppm higher in 2049-50. Punjab’s average available soil potassium keeps falling in case C because half of the net potassium removal is still drawn from potassium in the soil.
  • Covering the whole of the estimated 2023-24 net potassium removal with potash fertiliser would need about 616,000 tonnes of K₂O a year more than in 2023-24. Supplying 616,000 tonnes of K₂O would take about 20.5 million 50 kg bags of muriate of potash (MOP) at 60% K₂O. The same amount would take about 24.6 million 50 kg bags of sulfate of potash (SOP) at 50% K₂O.

Punjab’s alluvial soils are rich in the minerals mica and illite. These minerals hold potassium between their layers. As crops take out potassium that is not replaced, these minerals release some of the potassium they hold. Because of this release, available soil potassium falls more slowly than the potassium balance alone would suggest. The release, however, uses up the potassium held in the minerals. Weathered illite and vermiculite clays can also lock up part of any potash fertiliser applied later. Crops cannot use this locked-up potassium for a time (Wakeel and Ishfaq 2022).

6Effect on yield and fertiliser use

Potassium, crop yield and the uptake of nitrogen and phosphorus

In a 2-year wheat trial at the Adaptive Research Station, Mianwali, on sandy, low-potassium soil in the Thal, adding 38 kg of K₂O per acre (93 kg per hectare) as potash fertiliser raised grain yield by 25%, from 36.7 to 45.9 maunds per acre (3,625 to 4,533 kg per hectare; Abbas et al. 2013, Pakistan Journal of Botany).

Potassium also affects how much of the nitrogen and phosphorus in urea and DAP the crop takes up. The International Food Policy Research Institute (IFPRI) quotes a study on soil severely deficient in potassium. The study found that applying nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium together raised the share of each nutrient that the crop took up, compared with applying only 2 of them. For nitrogen, the share taken up by the crop rose from 16% to 76%:

NutrientShare taken up by the crop with 2 nutrients appliedShare taken up with nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium applied together
Nitrogen16%76%
Phosphorus1%13%
Potassium22%61%

In the nitrogen and phosphorus rows, “with 2 nutrients applied” means nitrogen and phosphorus only. In the potassium row, it means nitrogen and potassium only. Source: Haerdter and Fairhurst (2003), as quoted in Ali et al. 2016, IFPRI Discussion Paper 01516. The figures come from a single study, most likely on rice outside Pakistan. This report relies on the IFPRI quotation of that study. Its figures show what happens on soil severely deficient in potassium. They are an illustration and are not a Punjab average.

National figures for Pakistan show 2 trends that are consistent with the study above. Low available soil potassium is probably only part of the cause of the first trend. The 2 trends are as follows:

  • Producing 100 kg of wheat in Pakistan took 4 kg of fertiliser nutrients in 1980-81 and 7.9 kg in 2014 (IFPRI 2016).
  • For every kg of nitrogen applied as fertiliser, Pakistan applied 0.036 kg of potash (as K₂O) in 1985-86 and 0.007 kg in 2013-14 (IFPRI 2016).

On this evidence, where available soil potassium in a farmer’s field is low, the crop cannot fully use the nitrogen and phosphorus in the urea and DAP that the farmer has paid for.

7Recommendations for farmers, government and industry

Recommendations on giving potassium the same priority as nitrogen and phosphorus in soil testing, fertiliser policy and fertiliser supply

For farmers. Farmers should have their soil tested before the next sowing. Where the test shows available soil potassium below 100 ppm, especially on sandy land, part of the money spent on urea and DAP may be wasted until potash is added. On VAN’s 5-band scale, a result below 100 ppm is in the Weak or Critical band. A farmer with such a result can apply potash to a strip of the field and compare the crop on that strip with the rest of the field.

For the government. The most recent province-wide measurement of the yearly fall in Punjab’s available soil potassium that this report found was made on SFRI’s monitoring sites in 2003 to 2009. The government should restore fixed monitoring sites. It should re-test the sites every 2 to 3 years and publish the results. Fertiliser policy should give potassium the same priority as nitrogen and phosphorus.

For the fertiliser industry. Covering Punjab’s estimated 2023-24 net potassium removal would take about 616,000 tonnes of K₂O a year more than the potash fertiliser used in Punjab that year. Pakistan has no commercial potash mine. Supplying potassium on that scale would therefore need imported potash, potash recovered within Pakistan and products that farmers can afford.

8Conclusion

Potassium taken out and put back, the projections and the main uncertainty

Punjab’s soils still hold potassium. However, the available part that crops can use is being taken out faster than it is put back. Punjab’s estimated net potassium removal nearly doubled, from an average of about 328,000 tonnes of K₂O a year in 2003 to 2009 to about 616,000 tonnes in 2023-24. In the soil tests of about 2016 to 2018, the sandy Thal districts of Mianwali, Layyah and Bhakkar already had median available soil potassium below 100 ppm. If potash fertiliser use does not rise, the model projects that Punjab’s average available soil potassium will fall below the 100 ppm warning line in 2035-36 in the average case.

In the model, raising potash fertiliser use to 12 times its 2024-25 level delays the year in which Punjab’s average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm by only about 2 years. The delay is short because half of the projected net potassium removal would still be drawn from potassium in the soil. The continued fall in available soil potassium matters for farmers because a crop can take up less of the nitrogen and phosphorus in urea and DAP where available soil potassium is low. The main uncertainty is how strongly the release of potassium from soil minerals slows the yearly fall. No measurement in Punjab has settled this question.

9Method, checks and limitations

How the model works, how it was checked, and its limitations

The model in 4 steps.

  1. Crop output: the model starts from Punjab’s production of wheat, rice, sugarcane, maize and cotton in every year from 1971-72 to 2023-24.
  2. Potassium taken out: published studies give a range for the potassium in each tonne harvested. The fixed cases use figures at the low end of these ranges. The model runs draw from wider ranges. For wheat straw, or bhoosa, the range is 10 to 25 kg of K₂O per tonne of grain. For rice straw, the range is 15 to 25 kg per tonne of paddy. The share of rice straw removed from the field ranges from 10% to 50%. Bhoosa and maize stover are counted as removed from the field, because they are fed to animals. Rice straw and sugarcane trash are mostly burnt in the field. Their potassium therefore stays in the field. Half of the cotton sticks are counted as removed for firewood.
  3. Potassium put back: the model counts potash fertiliser in each year, farmyard manure, and canal and tube-well water. According to FAO, 1/4 of the farmyard manure reaches fields.
  4. Yearly fall: the model starts from the yearly fall of 3.52 ppm a year that SFRI measured on its monitoring sites in 2003 to 2009. That fall was about 1.5% a year of the monitoring sites’ average available soil potassium. Except in case A, the model raises or lowers this yearly fall each year in step with that year’s net potassium removal. Half of the model runs use case B’s rule of a yearly fall that is a percentage of that year’s available soil potassium. The other half use a floor of 60 to 80 ppm (Section 4).

Starting values. The projections start from the soil testing records of about 2016 to 2018. These are the most recent province-wide soil testing records used in this report. Weighted by each district’s cultivated area, the 2016 to 2018 average is 129.8 ppm rather than 132.3 ppm, because some districts with low available soil potassium have large cultivated areas. Cases A to C start from the unweighted average of 132.3 ppm. The model runs start from the area-weighted average of 129.8 ppm.

In the model runs, potassium in irrigation water, fodder removal and crop growth change the average-case year very little, because every run is scaled so that its own 2003 to 2009 net potassium removal reproduces the yearly fall SFRI measured.

Comparisons with measurements. The model’s results were compared with measured data in 2 ways. The first comparison looks at land measured 2 times. It is the only one of the 2 comparisons that tests the model. It is a weak test. Because the model is set to SFRI’s 2003 to 2009 yearly fall, only the Gujar Khan measurement in this comparison is a separate test. The second comparison looks at differences between districts. It cannot detect a fall over time. It therefore cannot test the model. It does show why districts differ.

The balance model gives a yearly fall in available soil potassium of about 1.5% a year at the 2003 to 2009 net potassium removal, and of about 2.8% a year at the larger 2023-24 net potassium removal. The yearly fall measured in Gujar Khan, about 2.3% a year, lies between these 2 figures. The Gujar Khan result is a weak test for 2 reasons. First, Gujar Khan is rainfed. Second, its first value is not a direct measurement. This report estimated that value from the share of samples in each grade reported by Qureshi et al. (2001).

Comparison 2: differences between districts. After cleaning, 769,578 of the 770,160 samples remained. These samples were compared district by district, after allowing for soil texture, organic matter and pH. If cropping alone were reducing available soil potassium, the districts where crops take out the most potassium per acre would have the lowest levels. Instead, districts where crops take out more potassium per acre have slightly higher available soil potassium. Each additional kg of K₂O per acre taken out goes with about 0.44 ppm more available soil potassium. This relationship is weak. It is also not statistically reliable. In the tests of about 2016 to 2018, Sahiwal, Multan and Rahim Yar Khan had high levels of available soil potassium, against low levels in Mianwali, Bhakkar and Mandi Bahauddin.

Much of the difference between districts comes from how each soil was formed. Parent material, river silt and groundwater set each district’s starting level of available soil potassium. The districts whose median was below 100 ppm in about 2016 to 2018 are therefore low partly because of how their soils were formed. Soil tests from a single period cannot separate that starting level from the effect of 50 years of cropping. For this reason, the yearly fall used in this report comes only from places measured more than once.

The model workbook is available from the author on request: info@van.com.pk. Every input is on 1 sheet with its source. The inputs that depend on judgement are marked. A reader can change those inputs.

Limitations of the model.

  • This report found only 1 province-wide measurement of the yearly fall in Punjab, made in 2003 to 2009. The US drawdown method gives the same yearly fall only because it is set to reproduce that measurement (Section 4). Scaling this yearly fall to the larger net potassium removal of recent years assumes that the yearly fall grows in proportion to the net potassium removal. The model runs test this assumption. Because mica releases potassium as available soil potassium falls, the projections that allow the least slowing by soil minerals may overstate the yearly fall.
  • The SFRI monitoring sites averaged about 240 ppm of available soil potassium, nearly 2 times the 132 ppm average of the soil samples tested in Punjab in about 2016 to 2018. How far their yearly fall applies to soils with lower available soil potassium is an assumption. The SFRI figures are taken from IFPRI 2016.
  • How strongly soil minerals slow the yearly fall is assumed, within a range. Half of the runs use case B’s rule that the yearly fall is a percentage of that year’s available soil potassium. Under that rule, the yearly fall becomes smaller in step with the level of available soil potassium. The other half use the assumption with a floor of 60 to 80 ppm. Weighting the 2 assumptions differently moves the average-case year anywhere between 2030-31 and 2037-38.
  • Irrigation water is the largest and least certain source of potassium put back. The potassium content of Punjab’s canal water is an assumption in the model. The fixed cases use the world river median of 1.3 mg of potassium per litre for canal water. The fixed cases also count half the potassium in tube-well water. The model runs vary these amounts (see the table above). Much of the potassium in tube-well water is the fields’ own potassium, leached and pumped back up. Only part of it is therefore a real addition of potassium to the field. In case B, irrigation water with more potassium than the world river median brings forward the year in which Punjab’s average available soil potassium falls below 100 ppm, to between the crop years 2024-25 and 2027-28. The year comes earlier because the amount of potassium put back in irrigation water is held constant in every year. The reasoning below compares Punjab’s recent net potassium removal, estimated at about 616,000 tonnes of K₂O in 2023-24, with its estimated average net potassium removal of about 328,000 tonnes of K₂O a year in 2003 to 2009. The reasoning has 3 steps. First, more potassium in irrigation water lowers the 2003 to 2009 net potassium removal and the recent net potassium removal by the same amount. Second, the recent net potassium removal therefore becomes larger relative to the 2003 to 2009 net potassium removal. Third, case B scales the yearly fall by the recent net potassium removal compared with the 2003 to 2009 net potassium removal. The yearly fall therefore becomes faster.
  • The model leaves out potassium leached from sandy Thal soils and potassium lost by erosion on rainfed land. The fixed cases also leave out fodder crops such as berseem, sorghum and maize fodder, along with potato, pulses, oilseeds and vegetables. The model runs add an uncertain allowance of 0 to 600,000 tonnes of K₂O a year for fodder and other removals. The real net potassium removal is therefore larger than in the fixed cases. Potato and vegetables receive much of the potash fertiliser applied. This potash is counted in the fertiliser figure. The potassium that potato and vegetables take out is left out of the count of potassium taken out.
  • Potassium put back from manure and irrigation water is held constant at recent levels for every year back to 1971-72. Net potassium removal in the early years is therefore understated.
  • The projected share below 100 ppm of the soils represented by the 2016 to 2018 samples is estimated by lowering every 2016 to 2018 sample in step with the projected average available soil potassium for Punjab. The share is therefore a modelled estimate.
  • Available soil potassium figures after 2016 to 2018 are modelled. Crop production and fertiliser figures up to 2023-24 are recorded statistics. The potassium taken out and put back is estimated from these statistics. 2 sets of figures assume that potash fertiliser use rises to 416,000 tonnes of K₂O a year by 2034-35. The first set is case C. The second set is the model runs in Section 5 that test this rise. Every other figure after 2023-24 shows what would happen if potash fertiliser use did not rise. These figures should not be read as predictions.

Data notes. Punjab Development Statistics for 50 Years gives wheat production in 2017-18 as 16,210 thousand tonnes, against 19,179 thousand tonnes in Punjab Agriculture Statistics 2024. This report uses the 19,179 thousand tonnes. For potash fertiliser use in 2017-18, Punjab Development Statistics for 50 Years gives 64,800 tonnes of K₂O, against 37,000 tonnes of K₂O from NFDC. This report uses NFDC figures from 2016-17 onwards. In the Sheikhupura soil workbook, the values were shifted 1 column against the headings. This report corrected that shift.
