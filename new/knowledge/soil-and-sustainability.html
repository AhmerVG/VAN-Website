Knowledge · the state of Pakistan’s soil

The state of Pakistan’s soil and the nutrients that crops remove from it

Published figures and calculations based on them show that, for decades, crops in Pakistan have removed more nutrients from the soil than have been put back. Every tonne of cane, wheat or cotton taken from a field carries nutrients away with it. When less is put back than the harvest removes, the difference comes from the soil’s own reserves. Those reserves are finite, even in soil that was once rich.

01Potash removed and applied

How much potash crops remove and how much is applied

Crops in Pakistan remove far more potash than is applied. Most of the potassium that the crops take up comes from the soil’s own reserves. Those reserves therefore fall every season. A 2017 review estimated that cropping in Pakistan removes 40 to 61 kg of potash (as K₂O) per acre a year. By comparison, the national average application in 2023 was 0.6 kg of potash (as K₂O) per acre of cropland. The 2 figures come from different sources and different years. Both figures give potash as K₂O, the form in which fertiliser statistics usually report potassium. Tahir Abbas’s Punjab potash balance model also finds that Punjab’s 5 main crops remove more potash than is put back in every year from 1984-85 to 2023-24.

Source · Removal: Wakeel, A. & Magen, H. (2017), Potash Use for Sustainable Crop Production in Pakistan: A Review, International Journal of Agriculture & Biology 19(3). The figures were divided by 2.471 to give figures per acre.

02Published soil surveys

Published soil surveys of organic matter and nutrients

Published soil surveys report low organic matter and widespread shortages of phosphorus, boron, zinc and potassium. The surveys’ figures cannot be compared directly with one another. The reason is that each survey has its own region, year, sample count and critical level. The critical level is the soil test value below which a survey counts a soil as deficient.

03How alkaline soil causes deficiencies

How alkaline soil causes nutrient deficiencies

Alkaline soil causes most of the deficiencies that the published soil surveys report. Most cultivated soil in Pakistan is calcareous and alkaline. These soils hold free calcium carbonate, also called lime, in every layer of the soil from the surface down. The pH of these soils is commonly above 8. Phosphorus applied to such soil combines with calcium into forms that the root cannot take up. Zinc, iron and manganese are micronutrients, the nutrients that are critical even though crops need them in small amounts. All 3 become less soluble as pH rises. Micronutrient deficiencies are therefore most common in the soils with the most lime and the highest pH. They are also most common where organic matter is lowest. On alkaline soil, part of the nitrogen in urea left on the soil surface turns to ammonia and is lost to the air before the crop can take it up.

Low organic matter makes each of the problems caused by alkaline soil worse. Organic matter does 4 things for the soil. It holds nutrients that water would otherwise wash below the roots. It keeps the soil’s pH from changing quickly. It feeds soil organisms. It improves soil structure and water retention. Where organic matter is below 1%, the soil gets very little of these 4 benefits. In Pakistan, organic matter is broken down faster than it is replaced, for 2 reasons. First, summer temperatures above 45 °C speed up its decomposition. Second, crop residue is usually burned or fed to livestock instead of being returned to the soil. As with the nutrients that crops remove, less organic matter is therefore returned to the soil than is lost from it.

04Soil nutrient records

How soil nutrient levels have been recorded in Pakistan

The soil nutrient measurements that VAN has found for Pakistan from the 1980s to the present come from surveys of particular places and years. These measurements are stated in parts per million.

Wakeel and Magen’s 2017 review of potash use in Pakistan notes that soil testing facilities in Pakistan are scarce. For this reason, the review notes, farmers in Pakistan usually do not have their soils analysed. Soil testing in Pakistan has been sparse and irregular. It has been organised district by district rather than as a national survey repeated at the same sites. The surveys therefore cannot serve as a consistent baseline against which to measure change.

The published evidence on depletion has 2 parts. The first part consists of the estimates of the potash removed by crops and of the potash applied. The second part consists of the separate surveys of the soil in particular places and years. Because the surveys cannot serve as a consistent baseline, the national trend in soil nutrient levels can only be calculated from these 2 parts of the evidence.

A national soil health atlas would allow the national trend in soil nutrient levels to be measured. The atlas would be based on a survey repeated on a regular schedule with a consistent method. It would publish its results for each district. Until Pakistan has such an atlas, every discussion of balanced nutrition in Pakistan, including VAN’s, relies on incomplete evidence. Balanced nutrition means applying each nutrient in the amount that the crop needs, after allowing for what the soil already supplies.
