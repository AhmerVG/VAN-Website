The national case

The nutrition Pakistan pays for

Pakistan applies more fertiliser per acre of cropland than the world average. Only part of the nutrient applied reaches the crop. VAN’s view is that Pakistan needs to shift from applying more nitrogen to balancing the nutrients it applies and improving the form, timing and placement of fertiliser. The aim of this shift is to raise the yield per kg of nutrient applied. VAN bases this view on 2 sets of figures. First, in 2023, Pakistan applied more fertiliser nutrient per acre of cropland than the world average and more than the United States (FAO). Second, VAN’s calculation from national figures shows that, between 1999-2000 and 2023-24, nutrient applied per acre of arable land rose faster than the yields of wheat, rice and cotton and the average yield of all cereals. The yield per kg of nutrient applied therefore fell for wheat, rice, cotton and all cereals. VAN’s reading is that these figures do not support the view that farmers need to apply more fertiliser to raise yields. However, the figures cannot show the effect of fertiliser on its own, because seed, irrigation, varieties and weather also changed over these years.

Pakistan’s nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) was 29.1% in 2023 (an FAO estimate). The 29.1% means that Pakistan’s harvested crops contained about 29 kg of nitrogen for every 100 kg of nitrogen added to cropland in 2023. The 100 kg counts nitrogen from all sources, including fertiliser and manure. In 2023, world NUE was 55% and United States NUE was about 71% (FAO estimates). This article calls the 29 kg the nitrogen “taken out in the harvested crop”. The other 71 kg of every 100 kg added to Pakistan’s cropland were not taken out in the harvested crop. Part of this nitrogen came from fertiliser that farmers had bought and applied.

01Usage

Pakistan applies more fertiliser per acre than the world average

In 2023, the United Kingdom applied 60.3 kg of fertiliser nutrient per acre of cropland and the United States 43.6 kg, both less than Pakistan’s 63.3 kg. Yet in 2023, cereal yields were about 72 maunds per acre in the United Kingdom and about 84 maunds per acre in the United States, against about 37 maunds per acre in Pakistan (FAO). In tonnes per hectare, the cereal yields were 7.1 in the United Kingdom, 8.3 in the United States and 3.6 in Pakistan. The fertiliser nutrient applied per acre is therefore not the main limit on Pakistan’s yields. Sections 3 to 5 show what happens to the nutrient after it is applied.

Of the countries shown when the chart opens, India applied the most fertiliser nutrient per acre of cropland in 2023, at 73.7 kg. Section 2 explains why India is a warning for Pakistan.

02Nutrient balance

The balance of nutrients in Pakistan’s fertiliser use

In 2023, nitrogen made up 78.5% of the fertiliser nutrient applied in Pakistan, phosphate 20.5% and potash 0.94%. A crop needs several nutrients besides nitrogen. The chart below compares Pakistan with India. India is Pakistan’s nearest large neighbour and grows similar crops on similar soils. India is shown as a warning for Pakistan. The note at the end of this section gives the reasons.

VAN’s calculation from FAOSTAT shows that, in 2023, Pakistan applied 83 tonnes of nitrogen for every 1 tonne of potash. This ratio is the same as 1.2 kg of potash for every 100 kg of nitrogen. Potash fertiliser supplies the nutrient potassium. In the plant, potassium helps build cell walls, move sugars and regulate water use. Of all the nutrients, potassium does the most to protect a crop against lodging, heat and drought. Lodging means that the crop falls over. When a crop receives a lot of nitrogen and little potassium, and the soil cannot make up the shortfall in potassium, the crop’s growth is soft and easily damaged. Such a crop also cannot use all the nitrogen applied. The national ratio of 83 tonnes of nitrogen to 1 tonne of potash suggests that many crops in Pakistan receive too little potassium to use all the nitrogen applied.

Why India is a warning for Pakistan

India’s nutrient balance is not one for Pakistan to copy. Of the countries shown in section 1, India applied the most fertiliser nutrient per acre of cropland in 2023. India’s Twelfth Five Year Plan called the design of its nutrient subsidy “seriously flawed” because it distorted the balance between nutrients (Planning Commission of India, Twelfth Five Year Plan, Vol. II, para 12.37). The Comptroller and Auditor General of India found that, between 2010-11 and 2013-14, urea consumption in India rose slightly while consumption of muriate of potash fell sharply (CAG of India, Report No. 16 of 2015). Muriate of potash is the common potash fertiliser. Muriate of potash is also known as potassium chloride. The same report shows that, between 2010-11 and 2013-14, the potash price in India rose and the urea price did not. India has held the retail price of a 45 kg bag of urea at 242 Indian rupees since 1 March 2018 (Department of Fertilizers, Government of India).

Pakistan should not follow India’s path of raising fertiliser use. In India, a urea price that did not rise, at a time when the potash price rose, has led to higher fertiliser use and a worse balance between nutrients. The urea price that did not rise has not led to a higher yield per kg of nutrient applied in India. Pakistan’s balance between nutrients is already worse than India’s. In 2023, potash was 0.94% of the fertiliser nutrient applied in Pakistan, against 6.13% in India.

04Where nitrogen goes

Where the nitrogen added to Pakistan’s cropland goes

How nitrogen is lost from the soil, and how alkaline soil binds phosphorus and zinc

Part of Pakistan’s nitrogen loss is caused by the chemistry of its soil. Farmers cannot control this soil chemistry. In Punjab, 69.1% of the 770,160 soil samples tested in about 2016 to 2018 had an alkaline pH above 8.0 (section 5). On such soil, urea left on the surface breaks down quickly to ammonia. This breakdown is called hydrolysis. Part of the nitrogen in the ammonia is lost to the air before the crop can take it up. The nitrogen that remains in the soil is converted to nitrate. Part of that nitrate moves with the irrigation water and is lost to water. In waterlogged soil, part of the nitrate is converted to nitrogen gases and lost to the air. This process is called denitrification. In alkaline soil, phosphorus and zinc also form calcium and carbonate compounds that the root cannot take up. High soil pH therefore causes 2 problems. It increases the loss of applied nitrogen. It also makes phosphorus and zinc in the soil less available to the crop.

NUE in Pakistan

Pakistan’s NUE was 29.1% in 2023 (an FAO estimate)

Of every 100 kg of nitrogen added to Pakistan’s cropland in 2023, 29 kg were taken out in the harvested crop (FAO). The 29 kg and 100 kg figures cover all crops in Pakistan together. FAO estimates that, of the same 100 kg, about 26 kg were lost to the air and 11 kg were lost to water. Part of this lost nitrogen came from fertiliser that farmers had paid for. The crop never received this nitrogen. A further part of the 100 kg was taken off the field in straw and stalks. The remainder is not accounted for. By comparison, NUE in the United States was about 71% in 2023.

Source · FAOSTAT, Cropland Nutrient Balance, December 2025 release (2023, FAO estimates). Shahzad et al. (2019), Nature Sustainability, ranks Pakistan lowest in its comparison set for nitrogen use efficiency as the study measures it.

Why the nitrogen is lost

Part of the loss is caused by soil conditions that the farmer cannot change.

About 69% of the samples in the Punjab soil testing programme, taken about 2016 to 2018, had a pH above 8.0. On such soil, part of the nitrogen in urea left on the surface turns to ammonia and is lost to the air before the crop can take it up. The same high pH also affects phosphate and zinc in calcareous soil. Calcareous soil is soil rich in calcium carbonate. In such soil, free calcium binds most of the phosphate applied. Zinc in such soil also becomes unavailable to the crop.

Part of the loss comes from application practices that the farmer can change.

How much nitrogen is lost also depends on where the fertiliser is placed, how the doses are split, whether irrigation follows the application, and what the fertiliser is mixed with.

Steps a farmer can take to reduce the loss

  1. 1
    Change to a coated urea.Ordinary urea releases all its nitrogen at once when it lands on hot soil. A coated urea releases its nitrogen over several days. A coated urea costs more than ordinary urea. This step is listed first because it has the largest effect. VAN bases this order on its own field trials, data and field experiments with coated urea. Published research also shows that urea which releases its nitrogen slowly loses less ammonia. In a meta-analysis of 120 studies on maize, controlled-release urea applied at the same rate as ordinary urea cut ammonia loss by 39.4% (Zhang et al. 2019).
  2. 2
    If you keep the same fertiliser, change how you apply it.When the same fertiliser is placed in a band beside the seed row, drilled at sowing, or side-dressed and then irrigated, more of its nitrogen reaches the crop than when it is broadcast on dry alkaline soil. Broadcasting means spreading fertiliser over the soil surface. Side-dressing means placing fertiliser beside the growing crop. When the farmer changes only the method, the bag and the price stay the same.Placement also matters for phosphate fertiliser, including diammonium phosphate (DAP). The closer a phosphate fertiliser is placed to the root, the less of it the soil binds before the root reaches it.The 5 application methods and what each one changes →
  3. 3
    If you apply fertiliser through the irrigation water, level the field first.Applying fertiliser through the irrigation water is called fertigation. On unlevelled ground, the water and everything dissolved in it reach the low end of the field first and the high end late or not at all. On a level field, fertigation spreads the nutrient more evenly than on unlevelled ground. This more even spread comes from levelling the field.
  4. 4
    Change the timing: split the nitrogen into doses.If all the nitrogen is applied in 1 early dose, much of it sits in the soil before the crop needs it. Nitrogen that sits in the soil can be lost. Split the nitrogen into doses that follow the crop’s own demand. Time each dose with an irrigation.
  5. 5
    Prepare the tank mix correctly.A correctly prepared tank mix helps the crop take up more of what is sprayed on the leaves or applied through the irrigation water. Most of the method is covered by these 4 rules.Fill the tank half full of water first. Keep the agitator running. Add products 1 at a time. Let each product disperse before adding the next. Adding products to an empty tank, or all at once, makes a mix curdle.Add products in order of how hard each is to disperse: wettable powders, then water-dispersible granules, then suspensions and flowables, then anything already in solution, then emulsifiable concentrates, and adjuvants and surfactants last of all.Jar-test any mix you have not made before. Mix the same products in the same order and at the same ratio in a clear jar. Leave the jar for 15 minutes. If the mix curdles, or forms flakes, a layer on top or sludge at the bottom, the mix will do the same in a 200-litre tank and block the boom.Spray the mix on the day you make it, in the early morning or late afternoon. A mix left standing separates. In the heat of the middle of the day, a leaf takes up far less, because the small pores on its surface close. These pores are called stomata.These are general spray rules. They do not show which VAN brands can be mixed together. VAN’s agronomy team answers, brand by brand, which VAN brands must not share a tank, at what concentration, and how your own water affects them. Companies that partner with VAN receive the full answers.

Steps 2, 3, 4 and 5 cost nothing. A farmer who changes only where and when the existing fertiliser is applied gets more of its nitrogen to the crop without buying an extra bag.

China and the United States

China

On VAN’s calculation from FAO and World Bank data, China’s total fertiliser nutrient use fell by 22.6% between 2014 and 2023, while its cereal yield rose by 8.9%. China issued a national programme in 2015 to stop the growth in fertiliser nutrient use. China’s total fertiliser nutrient use had already peaked in 2014. The programme therefore reinforced a fall that had begun before the programme was issued.

United States

On VAN’s calculation from World Bank and FAO data, the cereal production of the United States rose by 35% between 2000 and 2023, while its total fertiliser nutrient use rose by about 1%. On VAN’s calculation, United States cereal production per kg of fertiliser nutrient used rose from 20.8 kg in 2000 to 27.8 kg in 2023. The 2023 figure was 2.5 times Pakistan’s figure of about 11 kg on the same measure. This measure divides total cereal production by total fertiliser nutrient used. It therefore differs from the 23 kg of cereal grain per kg of fertiliser nutrient in section 3. That indicator divides cereal yield per acre by fertiliser nutrient applied per acre of all cropland.

The cases in this section point to 4 ways of raising the yield per kg of nutrient applied.

Balance

Balance means applying all the nutrients a crop needs instead of mainly nitrogen. Balance also means crop-specific formulations based on soil tests, in place of a single blend for every crop.

Uptake

Uptake means getting the crop to take up more of the nutrient applied.

Timing

Timing means splitting the nutrient into several applications timed to the crop’s demand, instead of applying most of it early in the season.

Placement

Placement means using deep placement, banding and fertigation instead of broadcasting fertiliser on the surface. These methods place the nutrient near the root, away from where it is easily lost.

In 2023-24, China still applied about 2.5 times as much fertiliser nutrient per acre of arable land as Pakistan, at 159 kg against 65 kg (World Bank, published as 394 and 160 kg/ha). The lesson from China is therefore about raising the yield per kg of nutrient applied while China’s fertiliser nutrient use per acre remains high.

07Application

How a nutrient is applied

The same quantity of a nutrient has different effects depending on its form, the time it is applied and the way it is placed. For example, urea broadcast on the surface of hot alkaline soil loses part of its nitrogen to the air as ammonia. The same nitrogen in a coated form is released over days. Less of the coated nitrogen is therefore exposed to loss at any one time. Timing changes the effect of a nutrient in the same way. A micronutrient spray adds to yield only when it is applied at the growth stage at which the crop needs it. At the wrong stage, the spray adds cost without adding yield.

In Pakistan, fertiliser is bought by the bag and by the nutrient percentage printed on it. The bag and the percentage printed on it do not tell the farmer when or how to apply the nutrient. These choices affect how much of the nutrient is lost.

09Evidence

How to read the evidence

The figures in sections 1 to 6 come from public sources: national and international statistics, and the Punjab soil testing programme. Ratios and indices are VAN’s calculations from those figures.

Peer-reviewed studies show that coated and controlled-release nitrogen reduces nitrogen losses compared with uncoated urea (Zhang et al. 2019). Coated and controlled-release nitrogen also raises the share of fertiliser nitrogen taken up by the crop (Zhang et al. 2019; Zhu et al. 2020). Peer-reviewed studies also show that elemental sulfur raises phosphorus availability in calcareous soil. These findings are about the technologies in general. They are not measurements made on VAN’s brands.

On request, VAN shares the results of any trial it holds on a specific VAN brand.
