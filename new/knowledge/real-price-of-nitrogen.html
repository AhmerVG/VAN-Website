The cost of low nitrogen use efficiency in Pakistan

Pakistan’s nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) was 29.1% in 2023, against a world NUE of 55%, according to estimates by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO). The 29.1% means that Pakistan’s harvested crops contained about 29 kg of nitrogen for every 100 kg of nitrogen added to cropland in 2023. The 100 kg counts nitrogen from 5 sources: fertiliser, manure, rain and dust, legumes and seed. This report calls the 29 kg the nitrogen “taken out in the harvested crop”. At an NUE of 29.1%, Pakistan added about 3.4 kg of nitrogen for each 1 kg taken out in the harvested crop.

This report values the 3.4 kg of nitrogen added for each kg taken out in the harvested crop at the urea price. The urea price is what 1 kg of nitrogen costs when it is bought in urea. In the Rabi winter crop season of 2024-25, the urea price was Rs 193 a kg of nitrogen, according to the National Fertilizer Development Centre (NFDC). This report calls the value of the 3.4 kg at the urea price the urea-price equivalent. The urea-price equivalent therefore depends on 2 things: the urea price and NUE. At Pakistan’s 2023 NUE and the Rabi 2024-25 urea price, the urea-price equivalent was about Rs 660 for each kg of nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop. The Rs 660 is a calculated value. The calculation prices all of the nitrogen added at the urea price, including the nitrogen from manure, rain and legumes. The Rs 660 is not a price that anyone pays. If Pakistan’s NUE reached Bangladesh’s NUE of 40%, the urea-price equivalent would fall by about 28%, from about Rs 660 to about Rs 480 for each kg of nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop. The 28% is calculated from the unrounded figures. This comparison holds the urea price at Rs 193 a kg of nitrogen and changes only NUE. As a result, the fall of about 28% would be the same at any urea price. The sections below set out the evidence on NUE in Pakistan, what other countries did, the approaches to raising NUE and the limits of the evidence.

Key findings
  • Pakistan’s NUE was 29.1% in 2023 (an FAO estimate). The 29.1% means that Pakistan’s harvested crops contained about 29 kg of nitrogen for every 100 kg of nitrogen added to cropland in 2023. Pakistan’s NUE fell from 54% in 1961 to 29.1% in 2023. In 2023, NUE was 40% in Bangladesh, 45% in Egypt, 47% in mainland China and 55% for the world.
  • In 2023, Pakistan added more nitrogen to its cropland than any country except China, India, Brazil and the United States. Of the 29 countries that added 1 million tonnes of nitrogen or more to their cropland in 2023, only Spain had a lower NUE than Pakistan. Per acre, Pakistan added less nitrogen than Bangladesh, Egypt or mainland China, at an average of 74 kg from all sources against 114 kg, 137 kg and 117 kg (183.4 kg per hectare against 281.4 kg, 338.2 kg and 288.6 kg). Pakistan also had a lower NUE than these 3 countries. As a result, about 22 kg of nitrogen per acre was taken out in Pakistan’s harvested crop, against 46 kg in Bangladesh, 62 kg in Egypt and 55 kg in mainland China (53.4 kg per hectare against 113.3 kg, 152.5 kg and 136.6 kg).
  • Of the 5.7 million tonnes of nitrogen added to Pakistan’s cropland in 2023, FAO estimates that 1.7 million tonnes were taken out in the harvested crop, 1.5 million tonnes were lost to the air and 0.6 million tonnes were lost to water. FAO estimates that a further 0.7 million tonnes were taken off the field in straw and stalks. VAN calculated the remaining 1.2 million tonnes by subtraction. This report calls the 1.2 million tonnes the nitrogen “not accounted for” (Section 3).
  • At the Rabi 2024-25 urea price of Rs 193 a kg of nitrogen, the urea-price equivalent was about Rs 660 for each kg of nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop. The nitrogen added for each kg taken out in the harvested crop can also be valued in wheat, at the market price of wheat. This report calls that value the wheat equivalent. The wheat equivalent was about 7.6 kg of wheat in 2023-24 and about 12 kg in 2024-25. The 7.6 kg and the 12 kg both use Pakistan’s NUE for all crops. The wheat equivalent rose because the urea price rose and the wheat price fell. The Rs 660 and the wheat equivalents are calculated from observed prices and FAO’s estimated NUE. They are illustrations of scale. They are not costs that anyone paid.
  • If Pakistan’s NUE reached Bangladesh’s NUE of 40%, the same harvest would need about 28% less nitrogen. Applied to Pakistan’s nitrogen sales of 2024-25, the urea-price equivalent of the 28% less nitrogen is about Rs 181 billion a year at the Rabi 2024-25 urea price. At the same price, each 1% less nitrogen has a urea-price equivalent of about Rs 6.5 billion a year. The Rs 181 billion and the Rs 6.5 billion are calculated values. They are not projected savings for farmers. The comparison with Bangladesh uses FAO’s NUE series. An older published series gives a different NUE for Bangladesh (Section 11).
  • In 2023, Pakistan used 1.2 kg of potash (K₂O) for every 100 kg of nitrogen, against 34 kg for the world. Potash is the fertiliser that supplies potassium. Potash matters to nitrogen because a crop growing in soil short of potassium cannot make full use of the nitrogen applied (Section 7). A study of 33 long-term rice-wheat experiments in the Indo-Gangetic Plains and China found that yields stagnated in most of the experiments (Ladha et al. 2003). The study’s authors wrote that depletion of soil potassium “seems to be a general cause”.
  • Mainland China used 20% less fertiliser nitrogen in 2021-23 than in 2014-16, comparing 3-year averages. Over the same years, the nitrogen taken out in China’s harvested crops rose 10%. Pakistan, by contrast, used 16% more fertiliser nitrogen over the same years. The nitrogen taken out in Pakistan’s harvested crops rose 9% in that time.

The national nitrogen figures are FAO estimates. 2023 is the latest year that FAO has published. The rupee values are urea-price equivalents that VAN calculated from NFDC prices and sales. They are a measure of scale. They are not a price that anyone pays or a saving that anyone is promised. Section 11 sets out the limits of the evidence.

1NUE in Pakistan and the world

How NUE is measured, and Pakistan’s NUE since 1961

In 2023, Pakistan’s NUE was 29.1%, against 55% for the world. NUE compares the nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop with all the nitrogen added to cropland. Nitrogen enters a field from 5 sources: fertiliser, manure, rain and dust, legumes that fix it from the air, and the seed itself. Some of this nitrogen is taken out in the grain, lint, cane and other crops that are harvested. The rest stays in the soil for a while, is taken off the field in straw and stalks, is lost to the air or is lost to water.

FAO’s Cropland Nutrient Balance records the nitrogen added to cropland and the nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop, for each country and each year since 1961 where data exist. NUE is the nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop divided by the nitrogen added. FAO’s NUE describes a country as a whole. It does not identify the best practice for any single field.

Pakistan’s NUE was 54% in 1961, peaked at 61% in 1970, was 34% in 2000, averaged about 28% over 2021-23 and was 29.1% in 2023. Pakistan’s NUE of 54% in 1961 came at a time when it used about 52,000 tonnes of fertiliser nitrogen a year. By comparison, world NUE was 55% in 1961, fell to 39% in 1988 and was 55% again in 2023.

In 2023, Bangladesh had an NUE of 40% and Egypt an NUE of 45%, both higher than Pakistan’s. Mainland China’s NUE rose from 34% in 2010 to 47% in 2023. China’s NUE of 34% in 2010 was about Pakistan’s NUE in 2000.

A low NUE does not mean that farmers are at fault. Part of the nitrogen not taken out in the harvested crop is lost because of soil chemistry. In the Punjab soil testing programme of about 2016 to 2018, about 69 in every 100 of the 770,160 soil samples had a pH above 8. These samples came from alkaline soil. Calcareous soil is soil that contains free lime. On hot, alkaline, calcareous soil, urea left on the surface converts quickly to ammonia gas and escapes to the air. Soil with low organic matter holds little nitrogen. Flood irrigation can also carry nitrogen below the roots in the form of nitrate. Another part of the nitrogen is lost because of practice. Practice means when, where and in what form nitrogen is applied. Section 9 sets out what the published trials show about practice.

2NUE among the largest users of nitrogen

Pakistan’s NUE beside the other large users of nitrogen

Of the 29 countries that added 1 million tonnes or more of nitrogen to their cropland in 2023, only Spain had a lower NUE than Pakistan. NUE matters most in these countries, because the tonnes of nitrogen they add are large. With 5.7 million tonnes, Pakistan added the 5th largest amount, after China, India, Brazil and the United States.

Per acre, Pakistan added less nitrogen than Bangladesh, Egypt or mainland China in 2023. Pakistan’s cropland received an average of about 74 kg of nitrogen per acre from all sources, against about 114 kg in Bangladesh, 137 kg in Egypt and 117 kg in mainland China (183.4 kg per hectare against 281.4 kg, 338.2 kg and 288.6 kg). Pakistan’s NUE was also lower than the NUE of these 3 countries. As a result, about 22 kg of nitrogen per acre was taken out in Pakistan’s harvested crop, against 46 kg in Bangladesh, 62 kg in Egypt and 55 kg in mainland China (53.4 kg per hectare against 113.3 kg, 152.5 kg and 136.6 kg).

A higher NUE is not always better. The EU Nitrogen Expert Panel set out NUE as an indicator. The panel set the desirable range of NUE at 50% to 90%. It treats an NUE below 50% as a sign that too much nitrogen is lost. Above 90%, there is a risk that the soil is being depleted. Above 100%, crops take out more nitrogen than is added. Ukraine’s NUE was 103% in 2023.

The panel’s NUE counts all nitrogen taken off the field. FAO’s NUE counts only the nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop. If the nitrogen in straw and stalks taken off the field is counted as well, Pakistan’s figure for 2023 is about 42%. This report calls that figure “NUE including straw and stalks”. Pakistan’s NUE including straw and stalks is still below the bottom of the panel’s range, which is 50%.

Pakistan’s NUE of 29.1% in 2023 was about 21 percentage points below the bottom of the panel’s desirable range. Of the 29 countries that added 1 million tonnes or more, 14 were inside the range, from Myanmar at 51% to Australia at 80%. Of the other 15 countries, 13 were below the range and 2 were above it.

World NUE of 55% in 2023 needs care as a benchmark, because it is an average that includes countries whose soil is being depleted, such as Ukraine at 103%.

Besides the range for NUE, the EU Nitrogen Expert Panel also set a limit for the nitrogen surplus. The surplus is the nitrogen added minus the nitrogen taken out. The panel put the desirable surplus below 32 kg per acre (80 kg per hectare). Section 8 compares the surplus of each country with this limit.

3Where the nitrogen added in 2023 ended up

Where Pakistan’s cropland nitrogen came from and went in 2023

Of the 5.68 million tonnes of nitrogen added to Pakistan’s cropland in 2023, 1.66 million tonnes were taken out in the harvested crop. Fertiliser supplied 3.79 million tonnes, or 67%, of the nitrogen added. Manure supplied 1.16 million tonnes, or 20%. Rain and dust, legumes and seed supplied the rest.

Beyond the nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop, FAO estimates that 1.49 million tonnes were lost to the air and 0.61 million tonnes were lost to water. Straw and stalks taken off the field as fodder, fuel or bedding account for 0.72 million tonnes. FAO does not count them in its NUE. VAN calculated the remaining 1.20 million tonnes by subtraction. This report calls the 1.20 million tonnes the nitrogen “not accounted for”. Part of the nitrogen not accounted for may stay in the soil. Another part is lost by routes that FAO does not list, such as conversion back to nitrogen gas in wet ground.

FAO gives loss to the air and loss to water each as 1 national figure for Pakistan, for all crops, provinces and products together. Neither figure divides the loss between soil and climate on one side and practice on the other. Section 9 sets out field measurements of loss at individual sites in Pakistan.

4Nitrogen added and nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop

Nitrogen added and nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop, 1961 to 2023

From 2000 to 2023, the nitrogen added to Pakistan’s cropland rose 72%, against 45% for the nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop. Over the longer period from 1961 to 2023, fertiliser nitrogen applied to Pakistan’s cropland rose to 73 times its 1961 level, from about 52,000 tonnes to 3.79 million tonnes. Over the same years, the nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop rose to about 6 times its 1961 level, from 266,000 tonnes to 1.66 million tonnes.

After 2000, both the nitrogen added and the nitrogen taken out rose more slowly than before 2000. The table gives the change for each item from 2000 to 2023.

These figures therefore cannot show how much of the growth in harvests came from nitrogen. They show only that the nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop grew more slowly than the nitrogen added.

5What the nitrogen added is worth at the urea price

The urea price and the urea-price equivalent

At the Rabi 2024-25 urea price, the urea-price equivalent of each kg of nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop was about Rs 660, a calculated value. The calculation starts from what a buyer pays for the nitrogen in a bag of urea. In the Rabi winter crop season of 2024-25, a 50 kg bag of urea averaged Rs 4,435 (NFDC). Urea is 46% nitrogen. A 50 kg bag of urea therefore holds 23 kg of nitrogen. Dividing the bag price by 23 kg gives a urea price of Rs 193 a kg of nitrogen. In the Kharif summer crop season of 2024-25, the urea price was Rs 204 a kg of nitrogen.

The urea price does not show how much of the nitrogen added is taken out in the harvested crop. At Pakistan’s NUE of 29.1% in 2023, about 3.4 kg of nitrogen was added for each kg taken out in the harvested crop. The urea-price equivalent puts a value on that 3.4 kg. It is the value of the nitrogen added when all of it is priced at the urea price. At the Rabi 2024-25 urea price of Rs 193 a kg of nitrogen, the urea-price equivalent is about Rs 660 for each kg taken out in the harvested crop. The urea-price equivalent is an illustration, because it also values the nitrogen from manure, rain and legumes at the urea price. The figures change if the nitrogen in straw and stalks taken off the field for fodder is counted as taken out. On that count, Pakistan’s NUE including straw and stalks is about 42%. At an NUE including straw and stalks of 42%, the urea-price equivalent is about Rs 460.

If Pakistan’s NUE were Bangladesh’s NUE, the urea-price equivalent would be about Rs 480 for each kg of nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop. At world NUE, it would be about Rs 350. Only NUE changes between the bars. The urea price is Rs 193 a kg of nitrogen in every bar.

NFDC also reports how many kg of wheat, at the market price after harvest, are needed to buy 1 kg of nitrogen in urea. The figure was 3.48 kg in 2024-25, up from 2.22 kg in 2023-24. It rose because the urea price rose and the wheat price fell. Dividing 3.48 kg by Pakistan’s NUE of 29.1% gives a wheat equivalent of about 12 kg for each kg of nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop. The 12 kg is an illustration, because FAO’s NUE covers all crops together.

6Nitrogen that would not be needed at a higher NUE, valued at the urea price

The urea-price equivalent of the nitrogen not needed at a higher NUE

At the Rabi 2024-25 urea price, each 1% less nitrogen for the same harvest has a urea-price equivalent of about Rs 6.5 billion a year. This figure is an illustration. It comes from applying the NUE arithmetic of Section 5 to Pakistan’s national nitrogen sales. Pakistan sold 3,387 thousand tonnes of nitrogen in 2024-25 (NFDC), down from 3,775 thousand tonnes in 2023-24. A higher NUE would let Pakistan grow either the same harvest with less nitrogen or a larger harvest with the same nitrogen. The limits of the figures in the table. The figures in the table are not savings that anyone is promised. They have 7 limits.

  • They value all nitrogen at the Rabi 2024-25 urea price. Some nitrogen is sold in diammonium phosphate (DAP) and other products, at other prices.
  • They assume that every source of nitrogen falls in the same proportion. If fertiliser fell and manure did not, fertiliser would have to fall by more than the share shown in the table.
  • They assume that the harvest stays exactly the same.
  • They combine 2024-25 sales with Pakistan’s NUE of 2023.
  • They use FAO’s NUE for Bangladesh. FAO’s series and an older published series give different NUE figures for Bangladesh (Section 11). For that reason, the table also shows the drier irrigated countries of Uzbekistan and Iran.
  • Their world row needs care, because world NUE of 55% is an average that includes countries whose soil is being depleted.
  • They count the value of the nitrogen only. Reaching a higher NUE would also cost money for better products, equipment, advice and time.

Because of these 7 limits, the figures in the table are a measure of scale only.

7Nitrogen and the other nutrients

Nitrogen, potash and phosphate in Pakistan’s fertiliser use

In 2023, Pakistan used 1.2 kg of potash for every 100 kg of nitrogen, against 34 kg for the world. Potash is the fertiliser that supplies potassium. The low use of potash matters because a crop takes up nitrogen together with the other nutrients. Liebig’s Law of the Minimum states that the nutrient in shortest supply limits the harvest. For that reason, if the soil is short of potassium, phosphate, sulfur or zinc, the crop cannot make full use of the nitrogen applied. The nitrogen that the crop does not use can be lost.

Pakistan’s fertiliser is mostly nitrogen. In 2024-25, the nutrients sold in Pakistan were 3,387 thousand tonnes of nitrogen, 919 thousand tonnes of phosphate and 44.5 thousand tonnes of potash (NFDC). By weight, these nutrients were 78% nitrogen, 21% phosphate and 1% potash. For every 100 kg of nitrogen in 2023, Bangladesh used 37 kg of potash and 69 kg of phosphate, China 36 kg and 38 kg, and the world 34 kg and 38 kg.

Across 33 long-term rice-wheat experiments in the Indo-Gangetic Plains and China, yields stagnated even at the recommended doses of nitrogen, phosphate and potash (Ladha et al. 2003). Rice yields stagnated in 72% of the experiments, and wheat yields in 85%. The authors wrote that “depletion of soil K seems to be a general cause”. K is the chemical symbol for potassium. In over 90% of the experiments, the potash applied was less than the potash taken out. The authors also named zinc and phosphate as causes.

Evidence from farms in India’s Punjab. On 56 wheat farms in Punjab, India, nitrogen, phosphate and potash were set to each field’s need and applied with better timing (Khurana et al. 2008). Wheat yield rose from 42 to 49 maunds per acre (4.2 to 4.8 tonnes per hectare). Nitrogen recovery is the share of the fertiliser nitrogen applied that the crop takes up. On these farms, nitrogen recovery rose from 17% to 27%. The study changed amount, balance and timing together. The effect of balance alone therefore cannot be separated. Although nitrogen recovery rose on these farms, India’s national nitrogen surplus still rose (Section 8).

In Pakistan, a wheat field study in Multan found that adding potash and zinc to nitrogen and phosphate raised nitrogen recovery by 230% compared with nitrogen and phosphate alone (Nawaz et al. 2024). The study was done at 1 site. It was run with a fertiliser company. The 230% is a relative change from a small starting value.

The Soil Poverty report on this site estimates the potash balance of Punjab’s soil (Tahir Abbas, 2026). Its figures come from a model. The report estimates that 5 crops in Pakistan’s Punjab took about 940,000 tonnes of potassium, expressed as K₂O, out of the soil in 2023-24, against about 36,000 tonnes supplied by fertiliser. Where the soil is short of potassium, part of the nitrogen applied is not used by the crop. Read Soil Poverty: Punjab’s potash →

8What other countries did

China, the European Union, the United States and India

China and the European Union reduced their nitrogen surplus. India’s nitrogen surplus rose while a large fertiliser subsidy continued. This section reports what these countries and the United States did, and what the data show. FAO’s figures and the figures of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) are built in different ways. The OECD counts all agricultural land, while FAO counts only cropland. This section therefore gives the 2 sets of figures separately. The 2 sets should not be mixed.

China set a national target after running a field programme. In 2015, China’s Ministry of Agriculture issued an action plan for zero growth in chemical fertiliser use by 2020. The plan set annual growth caps that fell from 1% to 0.4%. China’s national fertiliser use per acre peaked in 2014 and fell up to 2018 (Yu, Li and Doluschitz 2020). Its annual growth went from +1.1% a year in 2013-15 to −2.3% a year in 2016-18. On FAO’s figures, mainland China’s fertiliser nitrogen fell 20% between the 3-year averages of 2014-16 and 2021-23. Over the same years, the nitrogen taken out in China’s harvested crops rose 10%. China’s NUE rose from 37% to 47% between the 3-year averages of 2014-16 and 2021-23. Pakistan, by contrast, used 16% more fertiliser nitrogen over the same years. The nitrogen taken out in Pakistan’s harvested crops rose 9%. Pakistan’s NUE went from 30% to 28% between the 3-year averages of 2014-16 and 2021-23.

Before the target, from 2005 to 2015, a national campaign worked with about 20.9 million farmers in 452 counties on a cumulative 93.2 million acres (37.7 million hectares) of maize, rice and wheat. Average yields in the campaign rose by 10.8% to 11.5%, with 14.7% to 18.1% less nitrogen fertiliser (Cui et al. 2018). Price and market policies also changed over these years. The record shows these changes happening together. It cannot show which of them changed NUE. The record does show that the campaign reached about 20.9 million farmers with advice for each field.

On the OECD’s measure, the European Union’s nitrogen surplus fell 43.5% between 2000 and 2024, from 28.8 to 16.3 kg per acre of agricultural land (71.1 to 40.2 kg per hectare; OECD 2026, Table 12.32). The OECD also reports that the EU’s total factor productivity grew about 1% a year over 2014-23 (OECD 2026, Table 12.32). Total factor productivity is output relative to all inputs combined. On FAO’s cropland measure, the EU’s NUE rose from 43% in 2000 to 54% in 2023.

On the OECD’s measure, the nitrogen surplus of the United States fell from 13.9 kg per acre (34.4 kg per hectare) in 2000 to 11.7 kg per acre (29.0 kg per hectare) in 2024. The OECD describes this change as “better input management and reduced nutrient surpluses” (OECD 2026, Table 30.3). On FAO’s cropland measure, the NUE of the United States was 71% in 2023.

India is shown as a warning. India keeps fertiliser cheap with a large subsidy. The subsidy was INR 1.9 trillion (USD 23 billion) in 2023-24, after INR 2.55 trillion the year before. India has also made neem coating of urea mandatory. It gives advice based on Soil Health Cards. On the OECD’s measure, India’s nitrogen surplus rose 47% from 2000 to 2024, from 36.7 to 54.0 kg per acre (90.7 to 133.4 kg per hectare; OECD 2026, Table 14.3). VAN calculated the 47% from the OECD’s 2 figures. Mandatory neem coating, Soil Health Card advice and a subsidy that keeps the price low have not reversed that rise.

Pakistan’s nitrogen surplus per acre nearly doubled from 2000 to 2023. In the countries whose NUE improved, the nitrogen surplus was measured. A direction for nitrogen use was set. Advice and better practice were taken to farmers’ fields.

9Approaches to raising NUE

The 4 approaches to raising NUE

The published trials examine 4 approaches to raising NUE: technology, timing, placement and balanced nutrition. More than 1 approach is needed because Pakistan’s low NUE has more than 1 cause. The approaches are set out below in no particular order. Most of the published work was done outside Pakistan. The 4 approaches work together. However, the gains from different approaches cannot simply be added together.

Pakistani field measurements of loss vary with site and method. At Faisalabad, on irrigated land, a study traced the nitrogen applied with a nitrogen-15 balance (Mahmood et al. 1998). Nitrogen-15 is a labelled form of nitrogen that allows applied nitrogen to be traced. The study found that 39 kg of every 100 kg of nitrogen applied to maize were lost within 60 days. For wheat, 33 kg of every 100 kg applied were lost over the season. The authors attributed most of the loss to ammonia. At Peshawar, on alkaline soil with urea worked in and watered, the measured loss as ammonia was 12.7% of the nitrogen applied (Dawar et al. 2021). In a 2-season Pakistani wheat study on alkaline, calcareous soil, the measured loss as ammonia was about 4% of the basal nitrogen (Farooq et al. 2024). Basal nitrogen is the dose applied at sowing. The Faisalabad study measured total nitrogen lost. The Peshawar and 2-season studies measured ammonia lost to the air. These 2 measures should not be swapped for each other.

1. Technology: the form nitrogen comes in

Urea left on the surface of hot, alkaline soil is open to ammonia loss. Pakistani field measurements of this loss range from about 4% to 13% of the nitrogen applied, depending on the site and the method. 2 kinds of product can reduce this loss. Urease inhibitors slow the conversion of urea to ammonia. Controlled-release urea slows the release of nitrogen. Both kinds keep more nitrogen in the soil until the root can take it up. A third kind of product, the nitrification inhibitor, slows the conversion of ammonium to nitrate in the soil.

Not every technology suits alkaline, calcareous soil.

2. Timing: when the crop takes up nitrogen

A crop does not take up nitrogen evenly. Wheat needs little nitrogen in its first weeks. It needs most of its nitrogen as it tillers and grows. Tillering is the growth of side shoots. Nitrogen applied all at once at sowing stays in the soil until the crop needs it. Some of that nitrogen can be lost in the meantime. In the global synthesis, splitting the dose did not change ammonia loss. Where timing helps, it does so by matching the supply of nitrogen to the crop’s uptake. The trials below were done in India’s Punjab and northwest India. Although these trials raised nitrogen recovery or cut the nitrogen needed on the fields tested, India’s national nitrogen surplus still rose (Section 8).

SourceWhereWhat was measured
Singh et al. 2002Punjab, India, rice and wheatNeed-based nitrogen guided by a leaf colour chart or chlorophyll meter cut the nitrogen rice needed by 12.5% to 25% with no loss of yield.
Varinderpal-Singh et al. 2012, 6 field experimentsPunjab, India, irrigated wheatA schedule tied to irrigation applied 10 kg of nitrogen per acre at planting, 18 kg at the 1st irrigation and 12 or 18 kg at the 2nd, by leaf colour (25 kg, 45 kg and 30 or 45 kg per hectare). It gave high yields and better nitrogen recovery.
Bijay-Singh et al. 2017, 7 field experimentsNorthwest India, irrigated wheatSensor-guided nitrogen gave the same yield as a blanket 49 kg per acre (120 kg per hectare). Nitrogen recovery was 6.7% to 16.2% higher.
Khurana et al. 2007, 56 farmsPunjab, India, transplanted riceBetter timing and splitting raised nitrogen recovery from 20% to 30%. Yield rose from 52 to 61 maunds per acre (5.1 to 6.0 tonnes per hectare).

3. Placement: where the nitrogen is put in relation to the root

Ammonia escapes most easily from urea broadcast on the surface. Urea that is placed below the surface, drilled in or watered in soon after application is held by the soil.

4. Balanced nutrition: phosphate, potash and the other nutrients

A crop growing in soil short of potassium, phosphate, sulfur or zinc cannot fully use the nitrogen applied to it. Section 7 sets out the evidence. Of the 4 approaches, balanced nutrition is the one for which this report compares Pakistan’s use of a nutrient with that of other countries. In 2023, Pakistan used 1.2 kg of potash for every 100 kg of nitrogen, against 37 kg in Bangladesh.

Routes named for Pakistan. A Pakistani review of the country’s nitrogen record names 5 routes to a higher NUE (Shahzad et al. 2019). The 5 routes are balanced crop nutrition, irrigation management, legumes in rotation, precise in-season nitrogen management and enhanced efficiency fertilisers.

10What the evidence on NUE means for companies, policy makers and researchers

Points for companies, policy makers and researchers

For companies that make, sell or brand crop nutrition products. The urea price and NUE together decide the urea-price equivalent of each kg of nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop. Companies can add value by raising NUE through product development, advice and balanced nutrition. The value lies in supplying nitrogen in a form, at a time and in a place that suit Pakistan’s hot, alkaline fields. It also lies in supplying the potash, phosphate and micronutrients that the crop needs besides nitrogen. Most of the evidence on these approaches comes from outside Pakistan (Section 11).

For those who set policy. In the countries whose NUE improved, the nitrogen surplus was measured (Section 8). By contrast, the NUE figures for Pakistan in this report are FAO’s national estimates for all crops together. An official yearly NUE figure for Pakistan, with soil tests on fixed sites, would allow every later policy to be judged by whether NUE changes. However, a change in NUE alone would not show that a policy caused it (Section 11).

For agronomists and researchers. Pakistan needs multi-site field trials on farmers’ fields on 3 questions. The 3 questions are placement, timing relative to irrigation, and the effect of balanced nutrition on nitrogen recovery. These trials should use nitrogen-15 or full nitrogen balances. Their results should be published openly.

VAN’s view. Sections 1, 7 and 9 show that NUE in Pakistan depends on the soil, on the balance of nutrients and on when, where and in what form nitrogen is applied. For that reason, VAN’s view is that fertiliser development should begin with the soil, the crop and the conditions in which the nutrients will be used. In that view, the formulation of a fertiliser should follow from the agronomy.

11Limits of the evidence

The limits of the figures and the trial evidence

  • The national figures are FAO estimates. FAO marks every Pakistani nitrogen value in this release as an estimate, apart from the fertiliser figures for 1974 and 2000. FAO marks those 2 figures as official. VAN found no Pakistani agency that publishes its own national nitrogen balance. VAN also found no official Pakistani series of NUE by crop or by province.
  • Losses to the air and to water are FAO estimates. The 2 figures should be read as estimates rather than field measurements. FAO’s metadata for this release does not describe how loss to the air and loss to water are estimated.
  • 21% of the nitrogen added is not accounted for. VAN calculated the 1.20 million tonnes not accounted for by subtraction. Part of it may build up in the soil. Another part is lost by routes that FAO does not list. VAN cannot say how the nitrogen not accounted for divides between the soil and these other routes.
  • Straw and stalks are left out of NUE. FAO’s NUE counts only the nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop. FAO keeps the straw and stalks taken off the field as a separate item, for consistency with earlier releases. That item was 0.72 million tonnes in 2023. Counting it gives Pakistan an NUE including straw and stalks of about 42%. FAO leaves straw and stalks out of NUE for every country. As a result, countries that take more straw off the field for fodder, as Pakistan does, have a lower NUE.
  • FAO’s series and an older published series disagree on Bangladesh. For 2014, an older series gives Pakistan’s NUE as 27% and Bangladesh’s NUE as 28% (Lassaletta et al. 2014). FAO’s series gives 31% and 39% for the same year. Both series show Pakistan’s NUE as low. The comparisons with Bangladesh throughout this report use FAO’s series.
  • Fodder crops may be missing from FAO’s harvest figure. FAO builds its harvest figure from its crop production statistics. Those statistics may leave out fodder crops such as berseem. If they do, Pakistan’s NUE is understated.
  • FAO gives 1 NUE for all cropland. NUE will differ between wheat, rice, cotton, sugarcane and fodder. The wheat equivalent in Section 5 is therefore an illustration rather than a measurement for wheat.
  • The charts show association only. They show nitrogen, harvests and policies changing together. They cannot prove that any single cause changed NUE. Seed, water, varieties, weather, farm size and prices also changed over the same years. Only field trials measure the effect of a practice.
  • Part of the nitrogen loss cannot be avoided. Part of Pakistan’s loss comes from its soil and climate. That part of the loss will remain. VAN found no published measurement for Pakistan of how the loss divides between soil and climate on one side and practice on the other. VAN has not measured this split itself.
  • An NUE above 90% carries a risk to the soil. Above 90%, there is a risk that the soil is being depleted. Above 100%, the soil is being depleted.
  • The rupee figures are urea-price equivalents. They value all nitrogen at the Rabi 2024-25 urea price. They are not savings or forecasts. Reaching a higher NUE would also have a cost. The rupee figures leave that cost out.
  • Most trial evidence comes from outside Pakistan. The 4 approaches rest mainly on work in India, Bangladesh and China and on global syntheses. Pakistani field evidence exists for losses and for a few products. That evidence is too limited in scale to say how much each approach would change Pakistan’s national NUE. The published work does not rank the 4 approaches for Pakistan. VAN found no peer-reviewed Pakistani field trial on deep placement of urea, or comparing urea applied before a flood irrigation with urea applied after it. VAN also found no multi-site Pakistani trial that measures how much potash raises NUE.
  • The Soil Poverty report has not been peer reviewed. Section 7 uses its figures for the potash balance of Punjab’s soil. Those figures come from a model.

How the numbers were calculated. All FAO figures come from the bulk download of the Cropland Nutrient Balance, December 2025 release, for nitrogen. The NUE figures are FAO’s own. “Not accounted for” is FAO’s nitrogen surplus minus its estimates for air, water, and straw and stalks. The 29 largest users are every country with 1 million tonnes or more of nitrogen added in 2023. The count of 29 includes countries only. China is mainland China. FAO publishes its figures per hectare of cropland. VAN converted them to per acre at 1 acre = 0.4047 hectare. The urea-price equivalent for each kg of nitrogen taken out in the harvested crop is Rs 193 divided by 29.14%, Pakistan’s NUE before it is rounded to 29.1%. The urea-price equivalent of the nitrogen not needed is calculated as 3,387 thousand tonnes × (1 − Pakistan’s NUE ÷ the other country’s NUE) × Rs 193 a kg. The 3,387 thousand tonnes is Pakistan’s nitrogen sales in 2024-25. The ratios of potash and phosphate to nitrogen come from FAO’s fertiliser use per hectare of cropland, via Our World in Data.
