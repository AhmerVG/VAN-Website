Knowledge · Application systems

How a nutrient is delivered and how much of it reaches the crop

2 fields can receive the same nutrient, in the same quantity and in the same season. The crops in the 2 fields can still respond differently. How much of the nutrient reaches the crop depends on 4 things: the form of the product, the timing of the application, the placement of the application and the product’s physical fit with the application equipment. VAN therefore formulates each product for the application equipment as well as for the crop.

01Pivot, drip and drone

What each irrigation or spraying system requires of the product

A centre pivot, drip irrigation and a spray drone each place the nutrient differently. A centre pivot spreads it with the irrigation water over the whole circle, in many small doses. Drip irrigation delivers it to the wetted root zone. A drone delivers it to the leaf. By comparison, broadcasting spreads the nutrient over the whole soil surface. The roots must then reach the nutrient there.

In a centre pivot, the injected solution comes into contact with every part of a long galvanised span on each pass. The solution can damage the machine in 2 opposite ways. An acidic solution strips zinc from the galvanising. An alkaline solution attacks aluminium. Either way, the machine wears out sooner than normal. pH is therefore the main property to control when nutrients are applied through a pivot. VAN sets the pH of the product before it is packed, so the grower does not have to set it at the injection point.

The 2 corrosion risks to a pivot are described in standard corrosion engineering. The pH that VAN sets is measured for each batch and printed on the batch’s lab report. The lab report opens when the batch number is entered on the Verify a bag page.

02Form, timing, placement and fit

4 decisions that affect how much nutrient reaches the crop

Form

The form of a product affects how much of its nutrient stays available to the crop. On soil above pH 8, uncoated urea applied to the surface is broken down by hydrolysis, a reaction with water. Part of its nitrogen is then lost to the air as ammonia. By contrast, urea inside a sulfur coating dissolves gradually instead of all at once. Less of its nitrogen is therefore lost to the air as ammonia. Form matters for micronutrients too. In alkaline soil, chelated micronutrients stay available to the crop. The sulfate forms of the same micronutrients precipitate in alkaline soil and become unavailable to the crop.

Timing

A crop’s demand for nutrients peaks at defined growth stages. Much of the nutrient supplied outside those stages is wasted. VAN’s 28 crop plans are therefore written stage by stage.

Placement

A nutrient can be broadcast, drilled, side-dressed, banded, sprayed on the leaf or applied through the irrigation water. Application through the irrigation water is called fertigation. With each method, nutrient is lost in different ways and in different amounts. The method a grower uses usually depends most on the equipment that the grower owns, and also on agronomy. VAN therefore designs each product for the method by which it will be applied.

Physical fit

A product that blocks an emitter, settles out of a spray tank or is too coarse for a drone boom cannot be used with that equipment, whatever its nutrient content. Solubility, particle size, pH and behaviour in a tank mix decide whether a product can be used with a given piece of equipment.

05One Tank · ایک ٹینک

One Tank: products that VAN is developing for the 20-litre tank of a spray drone

A spray drone carries about 20 litres of spray mix. It then has to land. Everything applied to the crop from 1 tank load has to be dissolved or suspended in those 20 litres. The spray mix must reach the leaf without blocking a nozzle. It must also be safe on the leaf at a concentration that the labels of ordinary products do not cover. A product for a drone tank therefore needs a different specification from a product for a knapsack sprayer. VAN gives products made to this specification their own name: One Tank.

A drone sprays whatever spray mix is put in its tank, without correcting it. When a drone application fails because of the spray mix, the cause lies in a product grade that was not formulated for the tank.

Why One Tank products have their own name

VAN does not add the word “drone” to an existing brand name. A drone version of a VAN product is a different formulation. It keeps the nutrient analysis of the registered grade. It differs from the registered grade in its particle specification, its added delivery package and its application rate. If it carried the original product’s name, a grower might use one in place of the other. It is therefore given a name in a separate family, One Tank. The name refers to the 20 litres a drone tank holds.

VAN applies this naming rule without exception. Only a formulation designed from the start to be applied by drone takes a One Tank name. A product that already has a VAN brand name keeps that name and stays outside the family, even if VAN has since approved it for drone use, and even if it dissolves well. Sulfate of potash (SOP) also stays outside the One Tank family. SOP is a soil and fertigation product. VAN will not make a drone grade of SOP.

How the 20-litre tank limits the formulation

A One Tank application uses no more than 20 litres of total spray mix in the tank. VAN sets this limit before formulation work starts. VAN then decides everything else within the limit, including concentration, solubility and which products can be mixed in the same tank. A grade that needs more water than the drone can carry cannot be used as a drone grade, even if it works well in a boom sprayer.

The 20-litre limit has 3 direct consequences. First, the nutrient has to dissolve and stay dissolved, or stay in suspension, at several times the concentration used in a knapsack sprayer. Second, the spray mix has to keep passing through the nozzle screen for the whole length of a flight. Anything that settles or forms clumps in the tank is therefore ruled out. Third, the spray mix has to be safe on the leaf at this high concentration, because the same quantity of nutrient lands in far less water.

What VAN has decided so far for One Tank: the rate, the registration and the potash grade

The application rate for One Tank products is fixed at 1 litre of product per acre. 1 drone tank load of 20 litres of spray mix covers 1 acre. VAN’s position on registration is also settled. VAN treats a One Tank product as a formulation and grade variant of a VAN product that is already registered. On that basis, VAN holds that the One Tank product is covered by that product’s registration, with no separate licence. One Tank K is built on VAN’s potash grade VL-Potash.

A One Tank product has the same nutrient analysis as the registered VAN grade it is built from. That analysis is given on the grade’s own page. A One Tank product adds a delivery package to that grade. The delivery package is a set of ingredients that help the product spray at ultra-low volume. The product uses the same nutrient raw material as the registered grade. The delivery package is still in VAN’s research and development pipeline. Work on the package is at the stage of early small-scale field trials and the evaluation of their results.
