Knowledge · the 17 nutrients

The 17 nutrients a crop needs, and why the nutrient in shortest supply limits the harvest

A crop needs 17 nutrients. It takes 3 of them from air and water and the other 14 from the soil. The crop takes the 14 soil nutrients in amounts that range from tens of kilograms per acre down to grams per acre. Each nutrient has its own job in the plant, its own signs of shortage and other nutrients that it works with or against. Crops use these nutrients in the same way on every farm. A grower who understands how the nutrients work can make better fertiliser choices. This understanding matters because a crop given plenty of urea can still grow poorly. Liebig’s Law of the Minimum explains the reason. Under this law, crop growth is limited by the nutrient in shortest supply.

Nutrients from air and water

The crop takes carbon, hydrogen and oxygen from air and water. Together these 3 nutrients make up more than 90% of the plant’s dry weight. A grower does not buy them as fertiliser.

The Law of the Minimum · Justus von Liebig, 1840s

Liebig’s barrel: how the nutrient in shortest supply limits the crop

In the 1840s, the German chemist Justus von Liebig set out the Law of the Minimum. The law says that a crop’s growth is limited by the nutrient in shortest supply compared with what the crop needs. Adding more of any other nutrient does not raise growth above the limit set by that nutrient. The law is usually shown as a wooden barrel. The barrel is made of upright wooden planks called staves. Each stave stands for 1 nutrient. The water in the barrel stands for the crop’s growth. The barrel holds water only up to the top of its shortest stave. When nitrogen is not the shortest stave, another bag of urea makes the nitrogen stave taller. The water level still stays at the top of the shortest stave.

The 11 nutrients a grower manages

What each nutrient does, what a shortage looks like, and which nutrients it works with or against

N

Nitrogen

Major nutrients: the 3 needed in the largest amounts

Nitrogen is part of every protein and of chlorophyll. It determines how much leaf the crop grows and how green the leaves are. The leaves make the sugars that fill the grain.

When it is short
Older leaves turn pale yellow first, from the tip back, because the plant moves nitrogen out of old leaves into new ones. Plants stay small and thin. Grain has less protein.
Works with and against
The plant needs sulfur to turn nitrogen into protein. A crop short of sulfur cannot use all the nitrogen it is given. The nitrogen that the crop does not use is lost to the air or to water. A crop also needs potassium to make good use of nitrogen. Nitrogen drives leaf growth. Potassium then moves sugar out of the leaf. When a crop gets nitrogen without enough potassium, it makes soft, tall growth that falls over.
In Pakistan’s soils
Pakistan applies more nitrogen than any other nutrient. Pakistan loses a larger share of its applied nitrogen than of any other applied nutrient. The share of the nitrogen added to cropland that ends up in the harvested crop is called nitrogen use efficiency (NUE). Pakistan’s NUE was 29.1% in 2023 (FAOSTAT estimate). This NUE means that Pakistan’s harvested crops contained about 29 kg of nitrogen for every 100 kg of nitrogen added to cropland in 2023. The FAOSTAT estimate counts nitrogen added from all sources. About 71 kg of every 100 kg added was not taken out in the harvested crop. The form of urea is 1 factor in how much nitrogen is taken out in the harvested crop, because the form affects how much of the urea’s nitrogen reaches the roots. Sulfur coated urea (SCU) releases its nitrogen gradually over a period of days. With this gradual release, more of the nitrogen reaches the roots than with uncoated urea.

P

Phosphorus

Major nutrients: the 3 needed in the largest amounts

Inside the plant, phosphorus is used to move energy. The plant also needs phosphorus to grow roots. Phosphorus affects how fast a seedling puts down roots, how many tillers wheat makes, and how well the crop sets and fills its grain.

When it is short
Seedlings grow slowly and stay dark green. Some seedlings show a purple tint on the older leaves and stems. The crop makes fewer tillers, flowers late and forms small heads. Most of the damage is done in the first weeks. This early damage cannot be undone later.
Works with and against
Phosphorus and zinc work against each other. A heavy dose of phosphate on a soil already low in zinc makes the zinc shortage worse. Inside the plant, phosphorus needs calcium. In the soil, however, calcium binds phosphate into forms the root cannot take up. This binding is the central phosphorus problem in Pakistan’s soils.
In Pakistan’s soils
The Punjab soil testing programme tested 770,160 soil samples in about 2016 to 2018. In 35 of the 36 Punjab districts, the median available phosphorus in the programme’s samples was in the Critical or Weak band of VAN’s 5-band soil-test scale. In the same programme, 69.1% of samples had a pH above 8. Calcareous soil with a pH above 8 binds applied phosphate before the root reaches it. The Soil Atlas shows these readings for each district.

K

Potassium

Major nutrients: the 3 needed in the largest amounts

Potassium controls the movement of water and sugar in the plant. It opens and closes the pores in the leaf. It also moves sugar from the leaf to the grain, tuber or fruit. Potassium thickens the cell wall as well. Potassium affects crop quality, grain weight and the crop’s tolerance of stress. Heat, drought and disease all do more damage to a crop that is short of potassium.

When it is short
Older leaves scorch along the edge and the tip while the centre of the leaf stays green. Weak stems cause the crop to fall over. This falling over is called lodging. Grain is light. Fruit is small and stores badly.
Works with and against
Potassium works with nitrogen. Nitrogen drives leaf growth. Potassium then moves sugar from the leaf to fill the grain. Too much potassium reduces the plant’s uptake of magnesium and calcium. Potassium, magnesium and calcium should therefore be planned together.
In Pakistan’s soils
In the fiscal year 2024-25, potash (K₂O) was 1% of the fertiliser nutrients applied in Pakistan (NFDC). VAN prints this figure on its Crop Force bag. VAN’s wheat balance page also shows how much potash a wheat crop of 60 maunds per acre removes from the field when the straw, known as bhoosa, is also taken away.

Ca

Calcium

Secondary nutrients: 3 needed in moderate amounts

Calcium holds the cell walls together. The plant needs calcium at every growing point. Once calcium is placed in a tissue, it does not move again. The crop therefore needs a steady supply of calcium while it grows, especially where fruit and tubers are forming.

When it is short
The youngest tissue fails first. New leaves have burnt tips. Old leaves look normal. In fruit and tubers, calcium shortage causes blossom end rot in tomatoes, bitter pit in apples and hollow heart in potatoes.
Works with and against
Calcium and boron work together to build the cell wall. Boron is also needed for calcium to move to the growing point. Too much potassium or magnesium in the soil reduces the calcium the root takes up.
In Pakistan’s soils
Pakistan’s soils hold a great deal of calcium in the form of lime. A crop can still be short of calcium on these soils. 1 reason is that the calcium in lime is not in a form the root can take up. A heavy dose of potash also blocks the uptake of calcium.

Mg

Magnesium

Secondary nutrients: 3 needed in moderate amounts

Magnesium sits at the centre of every chlorophyll molecule. Without magnesium, the leaf cannot stay green or make sugar. Magnesium also helps the plant move phosphorus.

When it is short
Older leaves go yellow between the veins while the veins stay green. The leaves then have a striped or marbled look. The yellowing starts on the lower leaves and moves up the plant.
Works with and against
Too much potassium or too much calcium reduces the magnesium a crop takes up. When a fertiliser programme raises the potash dose without adding magnesium, the crop often shows a magnesium shortage in the same season.

S

Sulfur

Secondary nutrients: 3 needed in moderate amounts

Sulfur is part of 3 of the amino acids from which the plant builds protein, and of the oils in canola, mustard and sesame. The plant needs it to turn applied nitrogen into grain protein.

When it is short
Young leaves go pale yellow all over. Nitrogen shortage shows the opposite pattern and starts on the old leaves. Plants are thin and grow slowly. Oilseed crops give less oil.
Works with and against
Sulfur and nitrogen work as a pair. Without enough sulfur, extra nitrogen is wasted. Elemental sulfur also makes the soil around each sulfur granule more acidic. This acidity can release phosphate and zinc that the soil holds near the granule.

Zn

Zinc

Micronutrients: 8 needed in grams

Zinc is needed to make the growth hormone that lengthens the stem and the enzymes that build protein. It affects plant height, leaf size and, in rice and wheat, grain set.

When it is short
Young leaves come out small, narrow and yellow between the veins. The stem sections between the leaves, called internodes, stay short. The plant therefore looks bunched. In maize, a white band appears down the young leaf. In rice nurseries, brown spots appear on the older leaves.
Works with and against
Zinc and phosphorus work against each other. A heavy phosphate dose therefore makes a zinc shortage worse on a soil already low in zinc. At high pH, the soil binds zinc in the same way as it binds phosphate.
In Pakistan’s soils
The Soil Atlas shows the Punjab soil testing programme’s median zinc reading for each district. In several districts, the median zinc reading was in the Critical band. Before deciding on a zinc dose, a grower can check the Soil Atlas for the zinc reading of the district where the farm is.

B

Boron

Micronutrients: 8 needed in grams

Boron builds the cell wall together with calcium. It also carries sugar to the flower. Boron affects pollination, seed set and fruit set. When a cotton boll, a sunflower head or a rice panicle sets badly, boron shortage is often the cause.

When it is short
The signs are dying growing points, hollow or cracked stems, flowers that drop without setting, and misshapen fruit. Boron shortage also causes a hollow brown stem in cauliflower and a rotten heart in sugar beet.
Works with and against
Boron and calcium work together at the growing point. The difference between enough boron and too much boron is small. A grower therefore has to measure the boron dose carefully. For this reason, VAN coats boron onto its potash granules instead of relying only on a separate loose dose. Boron coated on a potash granule cannot separate from the potash in the bag. Every application of the potash therefore carries its boron with it.
In Pakistan’s soils
In the Punjab soil testing programme of about 2016 to 2018, 51.7% of samples were below Punjab’s working threshold for boron of 0.5 parts per million (ppm). The Soil Atlas shows the median boron reading and its band for each district.

Fe

Iron

Micronutrients: 8 needed in grams

Iron is needed to make chlorophyll and for the enzymes that carry electrons in the leaf. A leaf without iron cannot turn light into sugar.

When it is short
The youngest leaves go yellow between the veins while the veins stay sharply green. Later, whole leaves turn white. Old leaves stay green. Green old leaves distinguish iron shortage from magnesium shortage.
Works with and against
Iron and manganese compete for uptake. At high pH, the soil binds both of them. In Pakistan, iron shortage is usually caused by bicarbonate in irrigation water and lime in the soil. A soil can therefore hold plenty of iron that the root cannot take up.
In Pakistan’s soils
Iron is tested on VAN’s soil report and shown on the Soil Atlas. In 4 districts, the Punjab soil testing programme’s iron figures cannot be compared with those of the other 32 districts. The Soil Atlas notes that the figures from these 4 districts suggest a different laboratory method.

Mn

Manganese

Micronutrients: 8 needed in grams

Manganese splits water inside the leaf so that photosynthesis can run. It also helps the plant use nitrogen. Manganese strengthens the plant’s resistance to fungal disease as well.

When it is short
Young and middle leaves go yellow between the veins and show grey or brown speckles. In oats and wheat, the condition is called grey speck.
Works with and against
High pH and dry, well-aerated soil both make manganese less available. Too much iron or a heavy dose of lime makes manganese shortage worse.
In Pakistan’s soils
Manganese is tested on VAN’s soil report and shown on the Soil Atlas.

Cu

Copper

Micronutrients: 8 needed in grams

Copper is needed by the enzymes that build lignin, the woody part of the stem. Copper is also needed for pollen to form. Cereals short of copper produce empty heads.

When it is short
The signs are young leaves that twist and wilt at the tip, heads that emerge empty or half filled, and weak stems. Copper shortage is rare in Pakistan.
Works with and against
Organic matter holds copper tightly. Soils rich in organic matter, such as peaty and heavily manured soils, can therefore be short of copper while mineral soils are not.
In Pakistan’s soils
In the Punjab soil testing programme, the median copper reading was in the Healthy band in all 32 districts where the programme’s figure is comparable. The Soil Atlas marks the other 4 districts as not comparable.

The other 6 nutrients

The other 6 nutrients: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, chlorine, molybdenum and nickel

C
Carbon

The crop takes carbon from the air, through the leaf, as carbon dioxide. Every sugar, starch and fibre the crop makes is built from carbon.

H
Hydrogen

The crop takes hydrogen from water, through the root. Hydrogen is part of every sugar the leaf makes.

O
Oxygen

The crop takes oxygen from air and water. The root needs oxygen to breathe and to take up nutrients. Waterlogged soil holds little oxygen. A crop in a waterlogged field can therefore be short of nutrients even when the soil holds plenty of them.

Cl
Chlorine

Chlorine is needed in small amounts for the working of the leaf pores and for the plant’s water balance. Rain, irrigation water and muriate of potash (MOP) supply it. VAN’s soil report does not test for it.

Mo
Molybdenum

Molybdenum is needed in the smallest amount of all the nutrients. The plant needs it for the enzyme that turns nitrate into protein and for the bacteria that fix nitrogen in pulses. Unlike the other micronutrients, it becomes more available as pH rises. VAN’s soil report does not test for it.

Ni
Nickel

Nickel was the last nutrient to be recognised as essential for plants, in 1987. The plant needs a trace of it for the enzyme that breaks down urea in its tissues. VAN’s soil report does not test for it.
